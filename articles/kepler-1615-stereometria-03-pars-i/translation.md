## []{.page .unprinted n=10}Solid Geometry, Part I. The solid geometry of regular curved figures.

### Theorem I.

First of all, knowledge of the ratio of the circumference to the diameter was required. And Archimedes taught

that the ratio of the circumference to the diameter is very nearly that of the number 22 to 7.

![](/img/stereometria/p10-1.webp){alt="Schema I: a regular hexagon inscribed in circle CDB, with tangents at D and B meeting at F, and the line AF from the centre"}

To prove this he used figures inscribed in and circumscribed about the circle; and since these are infinite in number, for ease we shall use the hexagon. Let there be in the circle CBD a regular hexagon, whose angles are C, D, B and side DB, and let two straight lines touch the circle at D and B and meet at their common intersection F; and let the centre A be joined to F by the line AF, which cuts the straight line DB at G and the arc DB at E. Since, then, DGB is straight, that is, the shortest path from D to B, while DEB is an arc, that is, a path from D to B that is not the shortest, DEB is longer than DGB.

Conversely, since the straight line BF touches the circle, all parts of the arc EB lie inside FB, towards GB: so that if EB were straight it would certainly be shorter than FB. For the angles AEB, FEB are together equal to a right angle, since EFB is acute. So EB, subtending the smaller angle EFB, is less than FB, which subtends the greater. And we may argue about EB as about a straight line, because the force of the proof cuts the circle into the smallest arcs, which are equated with straight lines.

Meanwhile, from what is known to common sense, it may be accepted that the arc DEB, within the triangle DBF, is less than the lines DF, FB, since, though it bends towards the angle DFB, it has no particle outside the lines DF, FB; and by common sense what contains is greater than what is contained. It would be otherwise if the arc DEB were a winding and irregular line.

Since, then, DB is the side of the inscribed hexagon, and DF, FB are two halves of sides of the circumscribed hexagon, the arc DEB will be the sixth part of the circle; and it was greater than DB and less than DF, FB. Six lines DB, therefore, are less than the circumference of the circle, and twelve lines DF or FB are greater than the circumference.

Now DB, the side of the regular hexagon, is equal to the radius AB. Six radii AB, that is, three diameters CB, or (the diameter being divided into seven equal parts) twenty-one sevenths, are shorter than the circumference.

[]{.page .unprinted n=11}Again, since DG, GB are equal, GB will be half of AB. And the square of AB is equal to the squares of AG and GB together, and is four times the square of GB; so the square of AG is three times the square of GB. The ratio of the squares of AB and AG is therefore 4 : 3, and the ratio of the lines AB and AG is the square root of 4 : 3, namely that of the numbers 100000 and 86603. And as AG is to AB, so is GB to BF. So between GB and BF too the ratio is the square root of 4 : 3; so that, since GB is half of AB, namely 50000, BF has very nearly 57737 such parts. This number, then, taken twelve times, will be greater than the circumference of the circle. The sum is 477974, of parts of which the diameter has 200000. And of parts of which the diameter has 7, BD taken twelve times has 24 less one tenth. This number is therefore greater than the circumference itself; and the number 21 was less than it. And it is plain to the eye that the arc BE is nearer to BG than to the line BF. The circumference is therefore nearer to the number 21 than to 24 less one tenth. It is supposed, then, to depart from 21 by 1, and from the other by 2 less one tenth, so as to be, namely, 22. Archimedes proves this far more exactly with polygons, such as figures of 12, 24 and 48 sides, where it also appears that the circumference falls a little short of 22. Adriaan van Roomen proved by the same method that if the diameter of a circle is divided into 20000000000000000 parts,\
there are about 62831853071795862 such parts in the circumference.

### Addendum.

Of the three lines of the conic sections, called the parabola, hyperbola and ellipse, the ellipse is the rival of the circle; and I have proved in my commentaries on the motions of Mars that the length of the elliptical line is to the arithmetic mean between its two diameters, called the minor and major axes, likewise about as 22 to 7.

### Theorem II.

The area of a circle compared with the area of the square on its diameter has the ratio of about 11 to 14.

Archimedes uses an indirect proof, leading to an impossibility, about which many have said much. Its meaning seems to me to be this.

![](/img/stereometria/p11-1.webp){alt="Schema II: circle about centre A, its circumference divided into many small parts, and the triangle ABC whose base BC is the circumference laid out straight"}

The circumference of the circle BG has as many parts as it has points, that is, infinitely many; each of which is considered as the base of an isosceles triangle with sides AB, so that there are infinitely many triangles in the area of the circle, all []{.page .unprinted n=12}meeting with their vertices at the centre A. Let the circumference of the circle BG, then, be stretched out straight, and let BC be equal to it, and AB perpendicular to it. The bases of those infinitely many triangles or sectors will then all be imagined set side by side in the one straight line BC. Let one such base be BF, however small, and CE equal to it, and let the points F, E, C be joined to A. Since, then, there are as many triangles ABF, AEC on the straight line BC as there are sectors in the area of the circle, and the bases BF, EC are equal to theirs, and the height BA, which is also that of the sectors, is common to all, the triangles EAC, BAF will be equal, and each will equal one sector of the circle; and all of them together, having their bases on the line BC, that is, the triangle BAC made up of them all, will equal all the sectors of the circle, that is, the area of the circle made up of them all. This is what Archimedes' reduction to an impossibility means.

Then, BC being bisected at H, let the parallelogram ABHD be made, so that DH cuts AC at I. This parallelogram, a rectangle, will be equal to the area of the circle. For as the whole CB is to its half CH, so AB, that is, the whole DH, is to its half IH. So HI equals ID, and HC equals DA, that is, HB; and the angles at I are equal, and those at D and H right. The triangle ICH, which lies outside the parallelogram, is therefore equal to the triangle IAD, by which the parallelogram exceeds the trapezium AIHB.

Since, then, the diameter GB is supposed to be 7 parts, the square of the diameter will be 49. And since there are 22 of these parts in the circumference, that is, in BC, its half BH will have 11 of them, a little less, as above. Multiply 11 by the radius 3½, that is, by AB: the rectangle AH will be 38½.

### Corollary I.

The area of a sector of a circle (it is formed by straight lines from the centre and the arc they cut off) is equal to the rectangle under the radius and half the arc.

### Corollary II.

The area of a lesser segment of a circle (a part cut off by one straight line) is less than the area of the sector by the triangle contained by the straight lines of the sector and of the segment; the area of the greater segment is greater than its sector by the same amount.

For it is proved in geometry that the rectangle under the height of a triangle and half its base is equal to the area of the triangle. When the area of this triangle is taken from the area of the sector, therefore, the area of the lesser segment remains; when it is added, the area of the greater segment is formed.

In Schema I, DABE is the lesser sector, DABCD the greater; DGBE the lesser segment, DGBC the greater; DBA the triangle by which sector and segment differ. []{.page .unprinted n=13}It has two equal and congruent parts, AGB and AGD. So when the angle DAG is placed against GBA, and ADG against GAB, a rectangle is made of height AG and width GB. And in the doctrine of triangles GB is called the sine of half the arc EB, and GA the sine of its complement.

### Addendum I.

The circle has this in common with the parabola, that in both, portions cut off in any way by one straight line, if they have equal diameters, are also equal to each other in any one figure. Archimedes, *On Conoids*, IV.

### Addendum II.

The area of a parabola is four thirds of the area of a triangle having the same straight base as the parabola and the same height. Archimedes, *Quadrature of the Parabola*, Props. XVII and XXIV.

### Addendum III.

The area of an ellipse is to the area of a circle as the minor diameter of the ellipse to the major; and as the circle is to the square on the diameter, so is the ellipse to the rectangle of its diameters, that is, again about as 11 to 14. Archimedes, *On Spheroids*.

### Theorem III.

The ratio of a cylinder to the rectangular column of equal height that encloses its body with its square bases and parallel sides is the same as that of the circle to the circumscribed square, that is, the same as 11 to 14.

![](/img/stereometria/p13-1.webp){alt="Schema III: a cylinder standing inside a square column that encloses it"}

For as the cylinder's circular base CD is to the circumscribed square AB, so is CF, the body of the cylinder, to AE, the body of the rectangular parallelepiped or column.

Archimedes, *On the Sphere and Cylinder*.

For a cylinder and a column of equal height are here like plane figures given a body; the same things therefore happen to them as to the plane figures.

### []{.page .unprinted n=14}Theorem IV.

If a right column with parallel bases and a pyramid, or a cylinder and a cone, have the same base and the same height, the first will be three times the second.

![](/img/stereometria/p14-1.webp){alt="Schema IV: on the left a cone inscribed in a cylinder; on the right a pentagonal column ABCDE, FGHIK cut into prisms and tetrahedra"}

For every column, such as this pentagonal one BI, with parallel bases ABCDE and FGHIK and right angles BAF, AEK and the rest, can be resolved into its pentahedra or prisms: this pentagonal one into three, GHF, FHK, KHI, each enclosed by three parallelograms and two opposite triangles.

And a pentahedron is resolved into three tetrahedra, of which any two that divide one parallelogram face equally are of equal height.

Let the first prism, for example, be that under the triangles GHF, BCA. In the parallelograms BGFA and AFHC let the diagonals BF, CF be drawn from the angles B, C of the triangle BCA. These cut off a tetrahedron whose four triangles are BCA, BFC, BFA, AFC. There remains a four-sided pyramid with base BGHC and vertex F. The diagonal GC, then, cuts it into two tetrahedra: the four triangles of one are BGC, GFB, GFC, CFB; those of the other GHC, GFH, HFC, CFG.

Since, then, the parallelogram GHCB is bisected by the line GC, and the triangles GCH, GCB are equal, and the height GF the same (for BGF is a right angle), the tetrahedra GCBF, GCHF will be equal. Likewise the parallelogram GFAB is bisected by the line BF into the equal triangles FBG, FBA. And the plane BCA is at right angles to the plane GA; so the perpendicular from C to BA is the height of the tetrahedra GBFC and ABFC, and they are therefore equal. And GCFH was equal to the same GBFC. All three are therefore equal, and the prism HGA has three equal tetrahedra.

[]{.page .unprinted n=15}Now let a point L be taken in the upper base FGHIK and joined to the angles of the lower base ABCDE, so as to make a pentagonal pyramid. I say it is the third part of the column GAD. For the base ABCDE is divided by the lines AC, EC into three triangles as before, on which stand both the three pentahedra and the three parts of the pyramid, namely ABCL, ACEL, ECDL; and the perpendicular let fall from L to the plane ED equals the upright sides KE, FA and the rest. The height of the tetrahedra ABCF and ABCL is therefore the same, and they are equal. But ABCF is the third part of the prism ABC FGH; so ABCL is a third part of it too. Likewise the second part of the pyramid, ACEL, is a third of the second prism, and the third, ECDL, of the third. The whole pyramid, therefore, is the third part of the whole column, and the column three times the pyramid.

In the same way the cylinder MNOP, with parallel bases MN and OP, equals three cones MNQ, reaching with their vertex Q to the plane of the upper base and having the same base MN. For the same proof can be applied by analogy if you consider the circle, which is the base of the cylinder and the cone, to be divided from the centre into infinitely many triangles, on which stand as many prisms and as many parts of the cone, the former meeting in the axis of the cylinder, the latter in the axis of the cone.

### Theorem V.

The curved surface of the right-angled cone inscribed in a hemisphere is to its base, the great circle of the sphere, as √2 to 1, and is half of the base of the right-angled cone about the hemisphere.

![](/img/stereometria/p15-1.webp){alt="Schema V: a hemisphere on base BC with centre A, a right-angled cone BDC inscribed in it and a larger right-angled cone EGF about it, flanked by flowers"}

Let the sphere BDC be cut by a plane through the centre A, and let the section BC be extended, and let two right-angled cones be made: one wholly inside the hemisphere BDC, whose axis is the straight line AD from the centre A, perpendicular to the diameter; let this be BDC, having the base BC []{.page .unprinted n=16}and the vertex D in common with the hemisphere; the other wholly outside the hemisphere, touching it with the middles of its sides EG, FG, parallel to BD, CD, having its vertex G on the axis AD of the first extended, and its base EF in the plane that cuts the sphere. Since, then, BA and AD are equal, EA, the radius of the greater base, and AD, the height of the outer cone, will also be equal. And because EGF is a right angle, EG will be the side of the square circumscribed about the circle, and so equal to the diameter GC. But the square on EF is equal to the two squares on EG and GF, which are equal to the diameter BC; so the square on EF is twice the square on BC, and the circle EF twice the circle BC. I say too that the ratio of the conical surfaces to each other is 2 : 1, and the ratio of the curved surface of the lesser cone to the area of the base BC is √2 : 1.

For by what was said in Theorem II, the rectangle under the radius AB and the whole circumference BC is twice the area of the circle BC. And in the same way, by the same force of proof, the rectangle under the whole BD and the whole circumference BC is twice the curved surface of the cone BDC. So as AB is to BD, so is the plane surface of the circle BC to the curved surface of the cone BDC. But the ratio of AB to BD is 1 : √2, since the ratio of the squares is 1 : 2; so the ratio of the said surfaces is also 1 : √2. And since the cones are similar, as the plane base BC is to the curved BDC, so is the plane EF to the curved EGC; and alternately, as base to base, so curved cone to curved cone. The ratio between the bases is 2 : 1; so the ratio between the curved cones is 2 : 1.

### Theorem VI.

The convex surface of a sphere is four times the area of the great circle that cuts the sphere through its centre.

The proofs of Archimedes and Pappus are most ingenious, and not very easy to grasp; but the truth of the proposition and the first elements of a proof shine out from what has gone before.

For the convex surface of the greater cone EGF is greater than the surface of the hemisphere BDC, and that of the lesser BDC is less: the former covers the hemisphere, the latter lies wholly hidden beneath it. It is likely, then, that the surface of the hemisphere is the mean proportional between the surfaces of the two cones. But the lesser cone's surface is √2 times the area of the plane base, the greater's 2√2 times it; and the mean between √2 and 2√2 is 2. So it becomes likely that the surface of the hemisphere is twice, and that of the whole sphere four times, the area of the great circle BC. Archimedes proves it necessarily from similar principles.

### Theorem VII.

The convex surface of any segment of a sphere is equal to the plane circle whose radius is the chord of the segment's breadth from the pole to the base.

[]{.page .unprinted n=17}Segments of a sphere are here taken to be not any portions whatever, but only those made when the whole sphere is cut by one plane into two parts, so that each part has its own pole; for such a section makes the base of the segments a plane circle.

About the centre A let there be the surface of the sphere BDCL, and let this be its great circle; let the ends of the diameter DAL, namely D and L, be taken as the poles of the segments to come; and through the point I and through the centre A let two perpendiculars HIK and BAD pass, representing sections or circles at right angles to the plane DBL. Just as the whole DL, the chord of the semicircle LCDB from the pole D to the lowest point L (which point, in comparing the whole sphere with its segments, now stands in place of a base), becomes the radius of a plane circle equal to the whole curved surface of the sphere, by VI; so too DC, the chord of the half arc DKC of the segment BCD from the pole D to the base C, becomes the radius of a plane circle equal to the curved surface of the segment or hemisphere BDC, by V. So too, taking any segment whatever, such as HKD, the line DK, the chord of the segment's breadth from the pole D to the base K, becomes the radius of a plane circle equal to the curved surface of the segment KDH; and taking the segment HKL, whose pole is L and circular base HK, the chord KL becomes the radius of a plane circle equal to the curved surface of the segment KLH.

![](/img/stereometria/p17-1.webp){alt="A circle about centre A with diameter DAL, chords HIK and BAC, and lines from the pole D, set on a vertical axis between flowers"}

See the proof in Archimedes. Analogy will give you your first belief in it. For since this is so with the whole surface and with the half, it is probable that the same rule holds in the other segments too.

### Corollary.

![](/img/stereometria/p17-2.webp){alt="Four concentric circles about B, with the radii BK and BC marked"}

Segments of segments, at least those bounded on both sides by circular bases, are easily laid out flat by the same principles. Let BHKC be a segment of the segment BDC, whose bases are the circles HK, BC. First a circle is made equal to the greater spherical segment BCD; then another circle is made equal to the complement of the proposed segment of a segment. For this complement is the lesser spherical segment HKD, whether its pole is the same as before or another. So the difference of the plane circles DK, DC will equal the zone BHKC.

But other segments of segments, bounded not by whole plane circles but by parts of them, have as yet no known method of being equated or computed, unless the pole D and the axis DI of the whole segment HKI are placed at the meeting of the sections.

### []{.page .unprinted n=18}Theorem VIII.

The convex surface of a sphere and its axis are cut by a plane at right angles to the axis in the same ratio.

The seventh theorem serves geometrical construction; this eighth is more useful for arithmetic, and a most pleasant shortcut. That one produces a plane equal to the curved surface; this one shows lines equivalent to the segments.

In the preceding schema, if the whole axis DL is worth the whole surface DKLH, the part DI of the axis is worth the part HDK of the surface, provided DI is the axis of the section, that is, if it is at right angles to the cutting plane BK and cuts it at its centre I. So the part IL of the axis is worth the part HLK of the surface.

For HDK is to KLH as the circle on the straight line DK to the circle on KL, by VII, that is, as the square on KD to the square on KL. But the square on KD is to the square on KL as the straight line DI to the straight line IL. So as the line DI is to the line IL, so is the surface HDK to the surface KLH.

Analogy. Just as in the lines BA, HI lies the ratio of the circumferences BC, HK described about the centres A and I, so in the transverse lines DA, DI lies the ratio of the areas BDC, HDK bounded at those circles. And as DI is worth the area HDK, and DA the area BDC, so IA is worth the area of the zone BHKC.

### Theorem IX.

The surface of a right cylinder is equal to that of the sphere it encloses.

For the cylindrical surface is produced by multiplying the whole circumference KL (in the following schema) by the whole diameter KN or GB; and multiplying half the circumference KL by half the diameter AB produces a quarter of this, since similar figures are in the squared ratio of their sides.[^p18a] But the same multiplication produces the area of the great circle, by II, which is also a quarter of the spherical surface, by VI. So two fourfold multiples of the same thing are equal to each other, namely the curved surface of the cylinder KML and the curved surface of its globe or sphere GB, each four times the area KL.

### Theorem X.

The surfaces of a globe and of the cylinder that encloses it, cut off by the same plane at right angles to the axis, are equal.

In the following figure let GB be the globe and LN its cylinder, touching the globe in the middle and at G and B. And let the plane PST cut both surfaces: I say that the cylindrical zone KPSTL is equal to the curved surface of the segment GR of the globe.

It seems false, since the cylindrical surface is so loose and the surface of the globe draws in narrowly towards the top. But remember that by as much as the one is looser, by so much the other is broader, since it runs slantwise to the same height. And the proof is easy. Since KP and GR are equal, and the zone KRSTL is made by multiplying KP or GR into the whole circumference KOL, and likewise the zone PSTMN is made by multiplying PN or RB into the same whole circumference, then as the straight lines GR to RB and BG, so is the cylindrical surface KT to TN and NL. But as the straight lines GR []{.page .unprinted n=19}to RB and BG, so is the surface of the segment GR of the globe to the segment RB and to the whole surface BG of the globe, by VIII. So as the cylindrical KT is to TN and NL, so is the spherical GR to RB and BG. But the whole cylindrical surface NL is equal to the whole spherical surface GB, by IX. So the part KOL PST of the cylindrical surface will be equal to the part of the spherical surface in the segment GR.

### Theorem XI.

The body of a cylinder is to the body of the sphere it encloses in the ratio 3 : 2.

For the body of the sphere, by analogy with what was said in Theorem II, contains potentially within it infinitely many cones, as it were, meeting with their vertices at the centre of the sphere and standing with their bases, whose place is taken by points, on the surface. In Schema II, then, let BG be the sphere, A the centre, and AB, AG and infinitely many like them the cones, and these the smallest, that is, whose bases are the points B, G, and whose common vertex is A. Let a new schema be made, in which the curved surface of the sphere is stretched out into a plane circle whose diameter BC is twice the sphere's diameter BG, because the ratio of the circles is 4 : 1 by VI above; and let the circle be made on BC, and from its midpoint H let the perpendicular HD rise, equal to the radius AB. Let there be the cone BDC with base BC and vertex D: this cone will have a body equal to the body of the sphere BG. For the smallest bases B, G of all the infinitely many cones AB, AG in the sphere, together with the spherical surface in which they lie, are stretched out into the plane circle BC and set side by side; and where in the sphere they were right cones ABB, AGG, they have here become oblique cones DCC, DBB, except the middle one DHH, which stays upright; and they all have the same height DH and equal bases, being smallest ones; they are all therefore equal both to each other and to the right cones in the sphere; and the whole cone BDC, made up of them all, will be equal to the whole sphere BG made up of them all.

Multiplying the area of the circle BC by the radius HD, then, we produce the cylinder AICB, whose body is three times the cone BCD, by IV. But it is twice the cylinder that encloses the sphere, because it has the base BC, four times the base KL, and the height HD, half the height BG. For two cylinders AICB set one on the other would make a height equal to BG, the height of the cylinder KLB, and would be four times it, because of the base BG, four times the base KL; one AICB is therefore twice the cylinder KLB. And it was three times the cone BDC, that is, the body of the sphere BG, equal to the cone BDC. So if the cylinder AICB is divided into 6, half of it, namely 3, goes to the cylinder KLB, and a third of 6, namely 2, to the sphere GB. The cylinder KLB, therefore, is to its sphere GB as 3 to 2, which is the ratio 3 : 2.

![](/img/stereometria/p19-1.webp){alt="Schema VIII: on the left a cylinder KLMN enclosing the sphere GB; on the right a wide cylinder AICB on base BC, with the cone BDC inside it"}

[]{.page .unprinted n=20}The same can also be proved by IX, if we imagine the cylinder cut into infinitely many prisms meeting in the axis of the cylinder, having for their bases, which are quadrilateral, straight lines equal to the axis; and let all these bases of the prisms be set side by side in the curved surface of the cylinder, as we imagined something similar for the circumference of the circle above in Theorem II. In that Schema II, then, let the circle BG be each base of the cylinder, and let the circumference BG represent the curved surface of the cylinder, and the point A represent the axis of the cylinder, equal to the diameter BG; and let the curved surface of the cylinder be stretched out flat, and let it be BC, representing a rectangle whose length is BC and breadth BG; and let the axis of the cylinder AA be joined to the line CC, parallel to it, by the rectangle AACC, so that the prism AABBCC is equal to the body of the cylinder. For the smallest rectangular bases (that is, lines) of all the smallest prisms of the cylinder will be set side by side in the rectangular surface BC, and the prisms that were upright in the cylinder will here become slanting; but the edges of all of them will reach the end AA of the common height BA; they will therefore be of equal height both with one another and with the upright prisms of the cylinder itself. Multiplying the rectangle BBCC by the height BA, then, will produce a parallelepiped whose body is twice the body of the prism AABBCC, and so twice the body of the cylinder. But above, too, a circle equal to the rectangle BBCC, the curved surface of the cylinder (that is, four times the great circle, by VI), was multiplied by a height equal to the radius BA, and produced three times the cone equal to the sphere. So the same thing is three times the sphere and twice the cylinder; so, as above, the ratio of the cylinder to the sphere is 3 : 2.

### Theorem XII.

The body of a cube is to the body of the sphere it encloses as a little less than double, namely very nearly as 21 to 11.

For the cube STVX, in the following figure, is to its cylinder KLN which it encloses very nearly as 14 to 11, by III above, that is, as 42 to 33. And the cylinder KLN is to the sphere PGOB, which it encloses together with the cube, as 3 to 2, by XI above, that is, as 33 to 22. So the cube is to the sphere as 42 to 22, that is, as 21 to 11.

### Theorem XIII.

The body of a cone whose height is equal to the diameter of a sphere, and whose base is the great circle of the sphere, is half the body of the sphere.

For the cylinder KLN with the same base and height is three times its cone NGI, or as 3 to 1. And the same []{.page .unprinted n=21}cylinder is to its sphere as 3 to 2, by XI above. So the cone is to the sphere as 1 to 2.

### Corollary.

It is also clear from the same, further, that the first cone in the hemisphere (as the cone BDC of Theorem V) is to the cylinder enclosing the sphere as the octahedron in the sphere is to the cube about the sphere, namely a sixth part of the cylinder and a quarter of the sphere, and so half of its hemisphere and half of the cone described in Theorem XIII, since it has its whole base but only half its height.

But the greater cone EFG, in which the hemisphere lies (Theorem V), has an irrational or inexpressible body. For similar cones such as EFG and BDC are in the cubed ratio of their heights AG and AD. And the ratio of AG to AD is √2 : 1; so the ratio of the cones is (√2)³, that is, 2√2 : 1. And when one term of a ratio of 2√2 is stated (as when the cone BDC is called half the hemisphere BGD), the other term becomes inexpressible, except approximately.

### Addendum I.

The same is also the ratio of the body generated from an ellipse, called a spheroid, to the cone of equal height. Archimedes, *On Spheroids*, Props. XXIX, XXX.

### Addendum II.

As the spheroid is twice its cone, so the parabolic conoid is one and a half times its cone. See Props. XXIII and XXIV of the same book.

But the hyperbolic conoid (roughly the shape of a sore, a mountain, or an orderly heap of grain) brings the ratio 3 : 2 closer to equality. For to the lines that contain the ratio 3 : 2 (which are two and three times the line between the vertex and the centre of the figure) it adds the diameter on both sides.

### Theorem XIV.

A segment of a sphere is equal to a cone on the same base whose height is greater by so much that its excess is to the radius of the globe as the height of the segment itself is to the rest of the diameter.

[]{.page .unprinted n=22}Let there be the sphere CLB and its two segments HKD and HKL, and their axes DI, IL, parts of the diameter drawn through the centre A, which is extended from D towards O and P. A cone is sought equal to the lesser segment HKD, whose axis is DI and the rest of the diameter IL. Make, then, as IL to LA, so ID to DO: IO will be the height, and HK the base, of a cone whose body is equal to the body of the lesser segment HKD. Next let the segment be HKL, its axis LI and the rest of the diameter ID. Make again as ID to DA, so IL to LP: IP will be the height, and HK the base, of a cone equal to the greater segment HKL.

The proof is not for the general reader, so let anyone who wants it seek it from the second proposition of the second book of Archimedes *On the Sphere and Cylinder*; for it is not my plan to copy out the whole of Archimedes here. Its truth for the hemisphere, though, is plain as follows. Let there be the hemisphere BCD, whose axis is AD and the rest of the diameter AL. If I make as the rest of the diameter AL to the radius LA, so the axis AD to DQ, I make DQ equal to DA, and the height AQ will be twice the height AD of the segment. So I set the cone with base BL and height the diameter DL equal to the hemisphere. And that this is true you saw in the previous theorem, where this cone was half the sphere.

Archimedes also compares the ratio of solid segments with the ratio of the segments of the surface, proving that the ratio of the solids is less than the squared ratio of the surfaces and greater than its 3/2 power. In numbers a squared ratio is formed by multiplying each term of the ratio by itself, and the 3/2 power by multiplying the root of each term by the term itself. So if the surfaces of the segments were to each other as 4 to 9, square numbers, the bodies covered by those surfaces would be closer to each other than 16 and 81, each square being multiplied by itself, and further apart than 8 from 27 (the roots 2 and 3 of the squares being multiplied by the squares themselves), or 16 from 54. So if the lesser segment weighed 16, the greater would weigh between 54 and 81 pounds.

### Corollary.

The ratio of two segments of a globe to each other, in respect of their bulk, in any one section, is expressed in the heights of the cones, namely in IO and IP, just as before the ratio of the surfaces of the segments was expressed in ID and IL. But there is a great difference, in that the body of the whole globe in any one section is represented by its own particular line OP, whereas the whole surface of the globe is always represented by the same diameter DL.

### Addendum I.

Much here is common to the globe, the spheroid and the conoids. For as the section of a globe made by a plane is always a circle, so the section of a spheroid, not every one but that made by a plane parallel to the axis, is an ellipse similar to the spheroid; but ellipses of different and dissimilar kinds, or circles, result whenever a spheroid in any way, or a conoid through both opposite sides, is cut.

Archimedes, *On Spheroids*, XII, XIII, XIIII, XV.

### []{.page .unprinted n=23}Addendum II.

The rule for the segments of a spheroid is the same as for the segments of a globe if the section is at right angles to the axis; but if oblique, then one takes not half the axis but half of the line joining the vertices of the portions made (that is, their points highest above the section). For in the ellipse that generates the spheroid the axis is indeed among the diameters, but there are many diameters, of different lengths, each joining two opposite vertices.

### Theorem XV. Problem.

Just as Theorem VII made plane circles equal to the curved surfaces of segments, and Theorem VIII showed equivalent straight lines, comparable with one another for different sections: so too we can assign to the solidity of segments not only equal cones, as in Theorem XIV, but also equivalent planes, that is, planes of the same ratio and comparable with one another for different sections. I add this proof, though it is harder to grasp, because it has not been attempted by earlier geometers.

Three planes are sought having to each other the ratio that two segments of a globe have to each other and to the whole.

![](/img/stereometria/p23-1.webp){alt="The circle with diameter DAL and chords HIK and BAC, the same woodcut as on page 17"}

Let the segments be HKD and HLD. Then about the centre H or K, an end of the segment in the plane of the circle HDK, with distance HI or KI, let the arc IS be described, cutting HDK at S; and from S through A let a diameter be drawn, and let a straight line from K, KT, cut it at right angles, the intersection being R. This done, let three rectangles be made: the first under the radius AD and the height DL, or diameter of the whole globe, to represent the solidity of the whole globe; the second under the radius AD and the height DI of the segment, which will represent the solidity of the spherical sector HAKD; the third from AI (the height of the cone HKA) and RS, which I say represents in the same ratio the solid cone HIKA. So that, just as the cone HIKA taken from the sector HAKD leaves the segment HIKD, so the third rectangle taken from the second will leave a plane equivalent in the same ratio to the spherical segment HIKD.

But if the segment is greater than a hemisphere, namely HKL, then IL must be taken instead of DI in forming the second rectangle, and the rectangle under AI, RS must be added to it; and this will give a plane equivalent to the greater segment HIKL.

Proof. First, between the lines DI, IL and DL is the ratio of the segments of the surface and of the whole, by VIII. So between the rectangles too, []{.page .unprinted n=24}these lines being multiplied by the same radius AD, the ratio will be the same. But the sectors HAKD, HAKL and the whole globe have among themselves the same ratio as the surfaces HDK, KLH and the whole. So the rectangles made also have the ratio of the sectors HAKD and HAKL. Next, the height AD multiplied by a circular base equal to the surface of the segment HDK (whose radius is KD, by VII) produces a cylinder three times the cone equal to the sector HDKC; and the height AI multiplied by the circular base HK, whose radius is KI, likewise produces three times the cone HIKA. But as the circle of radius KD is to the circle of radius KI or KS, so is the square on KD to the square on KS; and as the square on KD is to the square on KS, so is the straight line DI to SR, by VII. So the ratio of the circular bases described with KD and RS is also the ratio of the lines DI, SR. But the ratio of cones is compounded of the ratio of their heights and the ratio of their bases, as Archimedes has it in *On Spheroids* XI. So the ratio of the sector HDKA and the cone HIKA in it is compounded of the ratio of AD to AI and of DI to SR. And the ratio of rectangles is likewise compounded of the ratios of corresponding sides. So the ratio of the sector HDKA to the cone HIKA in it is the same as that of the rectangles under AD, DI and under AI, SR. And therefore they are to their difference as the sector HDKA and the cone HIKA in it are to their difference, namely to the segment HIKA. This method concerns chiefly the cone HIKA, which is the common difference by which the segments differ from their sectors, and which it is sometimes worth having known.

### Comparative addendum.

So the segments of a parabolic conoid are to each other in the ratio of the squares of their axes. Archimedes, *On Spheroids*, XXVI.

### Corollary I.

Segments of segments of a sphere follow the same laws as regards their bodies as segments of the surface of the same kind did above, in the corollary to VII.

### Corollary II.

The zone of a sphere or spheroid, or ring-shaped body, contained by the middle part of the surface of the sphere or spheroid, called the zone, and a cylindrical surface within, is found as follows. Two equal segments are taken from the sphere or spheroid, and a cylinder of the same height as the zone, with the same bases as the segments taken away; and the body of the zone remains. But if the zone is not the middle of the globe or spheroid but inclined towards the pole or vertex, unequal segments are taken away, and a frustum of a cone, so that such a zone remains.

By a zone is meant one that has an even breadth all round, and under which is covered a conical frustum with parallel bases, whose measurements will now follow in the next theorem.

### []{.page .unprinted n=25}Theorem XVI.

A cone is cut in various ways: either through the vertex and the base, and that either by a plane or by the surface of another, smaller cone with the same vertex.

In either case, segments of the cone of equal height are to each other as their bases.

For the cone is here like a circle given a body; it therefore suffers from the section the same as the circle of its base.

Or the cone is cut by a plane parallel to the side by whose turning the cone was made; or by one not parallel, but one that would meet the side above the vertex, outside the figure. In either case indefinite portions are made, into whose solidity geometers have so far made no inquiry, since use did not require it.

Or the cone is cut by a plane through both sides, and two parts are made, the upper or vertical part and the lower frustum. The vertical part, cut by a plane oblique to the axis, Archimedes preferred to call a portion of a cone, recognizing no cones but isosceles ones; but Apollonius, making some cones isosceles and others scalene (an example of which is in Schema IV), recognizes such a portion too as a cone, however obliquely the axis is cut, provided the section is a circle.

As for the vertical segment, its section is an ellipse, which has its centre off the axis, towards the longer side of the cone. In Schema IX the axis of the cone GYN is GAB, whose vertical segment GNA has as its base an ellipse represented by the line NZ, whose centre or middle, where it is widest, lies below A, its intersection with the axis, towards N.

And this is always greater than the upright segment in which the plane parallel to the base NY passes through the end of the axis cut by the ellipse, namely through A. For the cutting plane, fixed at A and inclined to the axis GA, gains more of the body of the cone in the part AN than it loses in the part AZ.

Whether the cone whose base, parallel to the base NY, passes through the centre of the ellipse below A is equal to this segment GNA is worth considering; it is not yet clear to me, nor have I time now. See something on this below in the next theorem.

Meanwhile, Archimedes' *On Spheroids* XI is true in general of every vertical segment; by which the ratio of a cone or vertical segment to a cone is compounded of the ratios of their bases to each other and of their heights to each other.

So the area of the ellipse NA to the area of the circle NY is one part of the ratio of the solids GNA to GNY. And the ratio of these areas lies (by Addendum II to Theorem II) in the rectangle of the diameters of the ellipse and the square on NY, as better known. The other part of the ratio of the bodies is between GZ, the height of the segment GNA above the plane NAZ, and GB, the height of the cone NGY above the plane NY. Multiplying, then, the terms of the ratios crosswise, ZG by GB, and the rectangle of the diameters of the ellipse NA by the square on NY: as the latter product is to the former, so is the body of the cone NGY to the body of []{.page .unprinted n=26}the segment GNA. Each can also be computed separately; as GNA: multiplying the height GZ by the plane of the ellipse NA gives three times the body GNA.

In particular, if the cutting plane is parallel to the base NY, the ratio of the bodies, of the segment and the whole, will be the cube of the ratio of the heights or of the radii of the circular bases, or the 3/2 power of the ratio of the planes.

Because in this way the vertical segment and the whole cone become similar solids. Cubing each of the heights or of the radii of the bases separately, then, as the greater cube is to the lesser, so is the whole cone to the segment. Or if only the planes of the bases were known, their roots are sought and multiplied by them.

As for frustums, the method of finding them is easy from what has been said. For by one of the foregoing ways the cone as if whole is computed, and the missing vertical segment, from the given height or radius of the base. If the height of the missing part is not known, should it be needed, it is found by comparing the bases of the frustum with its height: for as the difference of the radii of the bases is to the height (if the frustum lies between perpendicular planes), so is the radius of the lesser base to the height of the missing segment. Taking the body of the missing segment from the whole cone, then, the body of the frustum sought remains.

But for those frustums contained between parallel bases there is again a shorter method, as before. For since the bases of such frustums cannot be unknown, their diameters (each by itself) are cubed, and the lesser cube is taken from the greater; then as the greatest is to the difference, so is the whole cone to the frustum.

Clavius teaches another way, more ingenious indeed, but also, as it seems to me, more difficult. For no less than above, the area of each base must be computed; and now there is the further step that the area must be found which is the mean proportional between the planes of the two bases. This is done either by multiplying and dividing lengthy numbers, or by two extractions of roots and their multiplication; then all three planes must be added, and the sum multiplied by the height of the frustum. Yet from the radii of the circles, which are needed to find the areas, whatever is sought can be had with much less trouble.

Since, however, frustums of cones made by a plane at right angles to the axis have many uses, and are above all necessary to the present matter because of the shape of barrels, I shall add here a most beautiful theorem and a most desirable shortcut; though for kinship's sake I put off the proof to the Supplement to Archimedes' Solid Geometry, Theorem XXII. The frustum is divided into a middle cylinder and a conical coat, as it were, put round it, which is a segment of the cone unlike those above, namely one made by a cylindrical surface. So if three diameters, two of the lesser base and one of the greater, are laid out in one straight line: then as the rectangle under the whole and the difference of the bases is to three times the square of the greater or lesser base, so is the body of the coat to the body of the greater or lesser cylinder.

[]{.page .unprinted n=27}So add twice the lesser to the greater, and multiply the sum by the difference of the greater and lesser, for the coat; then square the lesser and take three times the square for the inscribed cylinder; and the sum of this and that for the whole frustum.

For example, if the diameters of the bases are 3 and 5, the difference is 2. To twice 3 add 5: that makes 11, which multiply by 2: that makes 22 for the coat. And the square of 3 is 9, three times which, 27, is for the cylinder: so 49 for the frustum, in whatever proportion of height. But by the following theorem and its corollary this will become shorter still.

### Theorem XVII.

Upright segments of a cylinder, cut off by surfaces parallel to the axis, are to each other as the segments of the base.

For the cylinder is here like a circle or ellipse given a body; so the same happens to it here as to these figures in the base, in the same section. In Schema VIII the plane EFQX is parallel to the axis DH. So as the portion EFI or XQC of the circle is to the portion EFA or XQB, so is the portion FFIXV of the cylinder to the portion EFABX. See the corollary to Theorem II.

But segments made by a plane passing through the axis, provided it does not cut either base, are as the segments of the axis to each other.

For a right cylinder cut by a plane at right angles to the axis is like a line given a body, and indeed a cylindrical body; so the same happens to it as to the line. And other segments through the axis are equal to cylinders with the same length of axis. For as much as the segment lacks on one side of the cylinder of being upright, so much it gains on the other side; which does not happen in the section of a cone, because of its uneven thickness. In Schema VIII the planes PST, at right angles, and LSV, oblique to the axis GB, cut it together at R. So as GR is to RB, so are not only the upright portions KT to TN, but also the oblique LVK to VNL. For as much as LVK lacks in the part at L, namely LRT, so much it has over in the part at V, namely VRP, similar to LRT.

A segment of a cylindrical segment, such as is seen in Schema XIV, to follow, with the letters GST, contained namely by three surfaces, the plane semicircle GT, the plane half-ellipse GS, and the curved cylindrical VTS, so placed that the line where the planes cut falls perpendicularly on the point G of the axis HF: this segment, I say, is to the whole cylinder TY of equal height as 7 to 33, or about 14 to 66, and to the remaining segment HGS of the half-cylinder HGTS (cut off, that is, by a plane drawn through the axis HG) as 14 to 19. For this will be proved below in the Supplement to Archimedes for one kind of cylinder, namely when its height equals the circumference of the base, Theorems XIX, XX, XXI. But since not only is the whole globe equal to the whole half-cylinder, and the whole close ring to the whole segment of the cylinder where the vertices of the cutting ellipse touch the bases, but also []{.page .unprinted n=28}equal parts of the globe and the close ring, according to some aliquot part of the circumference to which they are proportional, are equal to parts of the segments of the said half-cylinder and cylinder according to the same aliquot part of the height, by force of the same laying out of round bodies straight; it follows that those segments of segments, of which the base of one is a circle and of the other a semicircle, are proportional to their heights, and so keep among themselves at every height the same ratio as the wholes, namely 7 to 33. But those whose bases are not a circle and a semicircle but other segments of a circle mix the ratio of the bases into this ratio of 7 to 33. So of these segments of cylindrical segments too the axiom holds, no less than of cones and pyramids, that those standing on the same base are to each other as their heights.

Finally, the outer rim of a cylinder cut by another cylindrical surface with the same axis is divided by a conical surface, whose bases are a wider circle below and a narrower one above, into two circular segments of a new kind, of which in the previous theorem we called the inner one the coat, namely of the narrower cylinder.

The ratio of these segments of the rim to each other, then, is that of the two arithmetic means set between the lesser diameter and the greater.

For dividing the number, say of the greater diameter, into two parts, the lesser diameter and the excess, and making squares of both the lesser and the greater, the greater square will contain four parts: I. the square of the lesser, II. the square of the difference, and III., IV. two rectangles under the lesser and the difference. Then, tripling the squares, there will be for the triple of the greater three squares of the lesser, three of the difference, and six rectangles. And these three squares of the difference with the six rectangles are the whole difference of the tripled squares. So as the squares are to their circles, and to cylinders of equal height, so is this difference to the whole cylindrical rim. But in the previous theorem we were told to multiply the difference by one greater and two lesser, that is, by itself and three lesser, for the inner part of the rim, which was named the coat. Taking away, then, one square of the difference and three rectangles from three squares and six rectangles, there remain two squares of the difference and three rectangles for the rest of the rim. And the ratio of three rectangles and one square to three rectangles and two squares is the same as that of three lesser with one difference to three lesser with two differences, and the same as that of one lesser with a third of the difference to one lesser with two thirds of the difference. But if to the lesser you add first one part, then two parts of the difference, you set up two arithmetic means between the lesser and the greater. And that is what we had proposed.

### Corollary II.

Segments of the rim of a greater cylinder about a lesser, made by a conical surface and standing on the same base, are, no less than the cylinders themselves, and so too the whole frustums between the same parallel circular bases, in the ratio of their heights.

And even if the upper base were not parallel to the lower, nor a circle, but an ellipse oblique to the axis, the two segments of the rim, outer and inner, are nevertheless in the ratio of those heights which the centre and the minor diameter of the ellipse have, the same diameter as that of the upright cylindrical base. So it is not for nothing that in the previous theorem, for conical portions cut off by a plane oblique to the axis, I sought the method of measurement from the greater diameter of the ellipse. Although the surface dividing such a rim of uneven height is not that of a right cone with the same axis as the cylinders, but of a scalene cone.

### []{.page .unprinted n=30}Address to my Patrons.

This little book, in manuscript, had lain hidden for sixteen months with a bookseller at Augsburg, to whom it had been commended by that eminent ornament of our Germany, Marcus Welser, a very dear friend to me while he lived; yet it was not printed, as had been promised me. At last, with difficulty, Welser being now departed from human affairs, I wrested my little book from the one who held it and took it back home.

From that time I resolved to print the little book at home, despite a great shortage of workmen; and this gave me the chance to send it out not only more correct but also larger than it was first written. Nor do I deny that some time has been taken from my other studies and spent on this inquiry; nor do I regret the loss. For it can by no means happen that labour should reap the fruit of immortality which has sown no seed of time.

And you, Illustrious and Noble Lord Baron Jörger, judged that what you heard of only begun, concerning figures untouched by Archimedes yet belonging most properly to the reckoning of barrels, should by no means be left out, but rather added to the work in the place []{.page .unprinted n=31}of an appendix.

I have indeed completed the greater part of them, so that little remains for perfection as regards the proofs, and nothing as regards their use; and I have put the treatise into the little book itself here, in its place, and joined it to the other parts in such a way that it may seem not an appendix or tail but the head or heart of the whole inquiry, as indeed it is. And this I believe both you, Lord Baron Jörger, and much more you, Most Illustrious Lord of Liechtenstein (whose constant exercise in this part of philosophy, and the excellent power of judgement you have acquired, I now know and see much more truly and fully than when I first addressed you in the preface of the book), will judge for yourselves when you have time to look into these things. Farewell and enjoy them. On the Ides of July, in the year 1615.
