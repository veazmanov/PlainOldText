## []{.page n=125}Preface.

Nature acts by ways so secret and so imperceptible in bringing towards the Earth the bodies we call heavy that, whatever attention or skill one uses, the senses can discover nothing of it. This is what led the philosophers of past centuries to seek the cause of this admirable effect only in the bodies themselves, and to attribute it to some internal and inherent quality that made them tend downwards and towards the centre of the Earth, or to an appetite of the parts to unite with the whole: which was not to set out causes, but to suppose obscure principles that are not understood. One may forgive this in those who were content with such solutions in many cases; but not so easily in Democritus and those of his sect, who, having undertaken to account for everything by atoms, made an exception of gravity alone, which they attached []{.page n=126}to terrestrial bodies, and to the atoms themselves, without asking where it could come to them from. Among the modern authors and restorers of philosophy, several judged rightly that something outside bodies had to be established to cause the attractions and the flights observed in them; but they went hardly further than those first ones, when they had recourse, some to a subtle and heavy air which, by pressing on bodies, made them fall (for this is already to suppose a gravity; and it is so contrary to the laws of mechanics to want a liquid and heavy matter to press down the bodies it surrounds that, on the contrary, it ought to make them rise, if they are supposed to have no weight in themselves, just as water makes an empty phial rise that is pushed down into it), others to spirits and immaterial emanations; which explains nothing, since we have no conception of how what is immaterial gives motion to a bodily substance.

Mr. Descartes recognised better than those before him that nothing more would ever be understood in physics than what could be referred to principles that do not exceed the reach of our mind, such as those that depend on bodies considered without qualities, and on their motions. But since the greatest difficul[]{.page n=127}ty lies in showing how so many different things are brought about by these principles alone, it is there that he did not succeed very well in several particular subjects he set himself to examine; among which, in my opinion, is that of gravity. This will be judged by the remarks I make in some places on what he wrote about it, to which I could have added others. And yet I admit that his attempts and his views, though false, served to open the way for me to what I have found on this same subject.

I do not give it as free of all doubt, or as something against which no objection can be made. It is too difficult to go so far in inquiries of this nature. I believe, however, that if the principal hypothesis on which I base myself is not the true one, there is little hope that it can be found while keeping within the bounds of the true and sound philosophy.

For the rest, what I offer here, in so far as it concerns only the cause of gravity, will not seem new to those who have read the Treatise of Physics of Mr. Rohault, since my theory is reported there almost whole. For that philosopher, having seen my experiment of the turning water and having heard the use I made of it (as he []{.page n=128}acknowledges candidly), found enough likelihood in my opinion to want to follow it. But because among my thoughts he mixes in some of those of Mr. Descartes and his own, and leaves out several things belonging to this subject, some of which he could not have known, I have been glad that it should be seen how I treated it myself.

The greater part of this Discourse was written while I lived in Paris, and it is in the registers of the Royal Academy of Sciences, up to the place where the alteration of pendulums by the motion of the Earth is discussed. The rest was added several years later; and after that again the Addition, on the occasion indicated at its beginning.
