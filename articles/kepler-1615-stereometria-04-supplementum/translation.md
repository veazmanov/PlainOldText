## []{.page .unprinted n=32}Supplement to Archimedes. On the solid geometry of figures that come next after conoids and spheroids.

Thus far Archimedes and the ancient geometers went, investigating the nature and measurements of regular figures, straight-lined and curved, and of the solids generated from them at the next step.

But since the shape of the barrel strays further from the regular figures, I thought it would be worth my while to set before the eyes, gathered as it were in one table, its generation and that of its relatives and the degrees of their kinship with the regular figures: both so that the proofs to follow should have more light, and also so that I might rouse the industry of the geometers of this age and, opening the gates of the most spacious field of geometry, proclaim by my announcement what still remains in it to be cultivated and explored.

### On the sections of the cone, the mothers of solids.

The conic sections of a curved surface that produce the various solids to be considered at this time are four curved lines, which I show here compared with one another in a schema. For every section of this kind is either a circle, CFE, or a parabola, PCQ, or a hyperbola, MCN, or an ellipse, CHI.

![](/img/stereometria/p32-1.webp){alt="Schema X: a circle, an ellipse, a parabola PCQ and a hyperbola MCN drawn through the common vertex C on axis CI, with the opposite hyperbola about S and the asymptotes RY, RZ"}

By reason of simplicity the order of these figures is this: first the circle, then the ellipse, then the parabola, and last the hyperbola. Of these the first two, the circle and the ellipse, return into themselves; but the two latter figures, the parabola and hyperbola, are so made that, if you continue them, they run out to infinity, always coming nearer and nearer to straightness, yet never wholly reaching it; and with this difference, that the arms of the parabola CP, CQ become more and more parallel to the single straight line CI, and so to each other, though meanwhile they move apart by an infinite distance; while the arms of the hyperbola CM, CN follow the course of two straight lines RY, RZ, which meet at R and from there move apart to infinity towards Y and Z; []{.page .unprinted n=33}which come ever nearer to the arms of the hyperbola, yet never meet them; from which property the lines RY, RZ are called asymptotes.

Again, of each pair there is one that alone fills its kind: of the finite ones the circle, of the infinite the parabola. But there are as many forms of ellipse as there can be forms of rectangles LK, and as many forms of hyperbola as of angles YRZ formed by the two lines RY, RZ. For as the circle CE is related to its uniform circumscribed square DB, and the parabola PCQ to the single axis or straight line CI, so ellipses CI are related to the infinite kinds of rectangles LK, and hyperbolas MCN to the infinite kinds of meeting lines YR, ZR, each to its own.

And note, for the drawing of the figures, that just as in the circle all the distances AC, AF, AE are equal to each other, so in the ellipse any two distances AC, OG, measured not from the centre E but from the two foci A, G, are equal to any other two, AH, HG, or AC, CG, or AI, IG. In the parabola, whose focus is A, if any line KI is drawn in the same plane at right angles to the axis GI, again the two lines AC, GI are equal to any other two such as AO, OK. In the hyperbola, whose one focus A is inside and the other T outside, with the similar figure VSX about it, the two distances of any point, such as N, from the foci A, T always have one and the same difference CS, the line between the vertices C and S, etc. See more a little below, on the number of solid figures.

### On the ways of generation.

Further, from the turning in a circle of these four plane figures (for we are not speaking of other forms at this time) are created infinitely many forms of solids, whose surfaces are not, like those of the cone and cylinder, straight in one direction, but curved in every direction, though more and less.

![](/img/stereometria/p33-1.webp){alt="Schema XI: five figures, numbered I to V, each a circle or segment turned about an upright axis CB, showing the kinds of solid made by the turning"}

The kinds of turning are in general five, but they are later subdivided more finely when we come to more compound figures. In the adjoining schema these kinds are shown roughly. For I. the axis CB about which the figure is to be turned either stands further from the centre F of the figure ED to be turned than to meet the figure. Then the figure DE, turned straight round in the circle FCG with its breadth upright, []{.page .unprinted n=34}makes a ring, with a space in the middle whose centre is A. II. Or this axis of generation, such as AB, touches the figure FD to be turned; let it touch at A, and, the point A of the circumference staying fixed, let the whole circle be turned about CB: then figures are generated that you might call close rings. III. Or the axis of generation cuts the figure to be turned off its middle, as if the greater segment MDN of a circle is to be turned about its chord MN, so that the centre F passes through G; and a figure is generated hollow in two opposite places, namely about M and N, which is the shape of the apple, the fruit of the tree. IV. Or the axis of generation passes through the very centre of the figure, as if the semicircle CDB is to be turned about the fixed diameter CAB; and then a sphere or perfect globe is generated. V. Or finally the axis of generation cuts the figure to be turned within its middle, as if the remaining or lesser segment CDE of the circle MDN is to be turned about its chord CAD; and then a figure is generated pointed in two opposite places, which you might name from the shape of the lemon.

### On the number and differences of the figures.

If the three remaining figures, which arise from the section of a cone, were as simple as the circle we have been speaking of, the solid figures produced in these five ways would be twenty in all, five from each of them, as so far from the circle, for the five ways of circular creation. But because of the mixed nature of the remaining figures, this number of twenty solids is extended to ninety.

For since the three figures properly called conic are not alike all round, it happens that what for the circle is a single line, taken as the axis of generation, becomes several for these figures, and a single point in the circle becomes several points in the others. In the circle the vertex is spoken of in one and the same way, and any point of the circumference can be taken as the vertex. In the others the primary vertex is nothing but the end of a certain line, namely that called the axis, which in the two following figures is CI, whose vertices are represented by C; but vertices in a wider sense are as many as the lines chosen in the figure for the axis of generation, or parallel to it; which we may call vertices of position, because when such a line is set upright, the highest point of the whole figure becomes its vertex. So in the following Schema XIII, if the diameter OS is chosen for the axis of generation, A becomes the vertex of position; whereas if the tangent EF is chosen as the axis of generation, there is either no vertex, namely in obtuse-angled hyperbolas in a certain position, or one far other than A, say about O. In the circle the centre is the same as the focus, namely A in Schema X above. In the parabola there is one focus A, but no centre; unless you imagine its centre to have withdrawn to an infinite distance, and the other focus to twice that distance, along the line CI from the vertex C. In the ellipse there is both a centre E and two foci A, G, all within the figure. In the hyperbola likewise a centre and two foci; but the centre is outside the figure at R, and of the foci one is inside, at A, the other outside, at T, which is inside the opposite section VSX. In the circle all diameters serve as axes; in the others the axis is often one thing and the diameter another, as species is one thing and genus another.

[]{.page .unprinted n=35}And in the parabola (to return to Schema XIII following) all the diameters are equidistant, such as CI, OS, HA; but in the hyperbola and the ellipse they all pass through the centre of the figure R, which in the hyperbola is outside, in the ellipse inside in the middle. In the circle all the diameters are equal; in the ellipse they differ in length, as CI, OS; in the parabola and hyperbola they have no finite length. In the circle and the ellipse every line touching the figure has a diameter equidistant from it; in the parabola and hyperbola a line equidistant from any tangent, as PK from FE, cannot be a diameter of the figure. According to the difference of these points and lines, then, the plane figures, when turned, produce kinds of solid that are different too, always in fact and mostly also to the eye; which I shall not be reluctant to go through one by one, without, however, repeating the five kinds arising from the circular section of the cone, each of which leads a family of those that follow, while some have followers in common.

Let us begin with those at which Archimedes stopped. In the adjoining schema let the axis of the figure be CI, about which the figure PCQ is turned, so that, CI staying fixed, the point N passes through the point O, and Q through P. Two conoid solids are thus created, the parabolic and the hyperbolic, and the long spheroid, or egg shape, whose vertices are C, I and widest circle ERK; on which Archimedes wrote in his book *On Conoids and Spheroids*. The way of generation is that of number IV, the same as the globe's. There are three kinds, by the number of the lines. \[1. 2. 3.\]

![](/img/stereometria/p35-1.webp){alt="Schema XII: a parabola and a hyperbola with vertex C on axis CI, and below them ellipses turned about their axes into egg-shaped solids, drawn with lines of latitude and longitude"}

Now let the axis of generation be not the axis CI of the figure itself but one parallel to it. It will then be either outside the figure, as at DF, or touching it, as LH touching at E: both of which have place only in the ellipse. For in the others every line parallel to the axis in the same plane cuts the figure when continued. In the ellipse, then, DF staying fixed and the whole figure NOPQ being carried round at the distance RG of the centre, a kind of ring is generated that you might call a tall ring: it is like the garland of country girls, since it has a space in the middle. So, LH staying fixed and the point of contact E, the whole figure NOPQ likewise being carried round, a solid of the same name results, but with no space in the middle, that is, a close ring. Imagine both in Schema XI at numbers I and II, but understand upright ellipses in place of the circular sections. \[4. 5.\]

[]{.page .unprinted n=36}Or the axis of turning will be inside the figure; let it be OK, and let it lie either on this side of the axis CI of the figure, so that the axis CI with the greater portion OCQ of the figure is turned about OK, and, O staying fixed, the vertex C passes through S and Q through V: then the parabola and hyperbola, the latter more so, make the figures QCOSV, like Mount Etna because of the hollow in its summit, which the Greeks celebrate with the word crater; but the ellipse in this way creates a figure like the quince, which you will imagine in Schema XI above at number III, but understanding, in place of the joined circles of the section, joined upright ellipses, as in Schema XII. \[6. 7. 8.\]

Let the said OK also lie beyond the axis CI of the figure, parallel to it, so that the lesser part PO of the figure, which does not have the axis, is to be turned about the fixed OK: then from the parabola and hyperbola certain kinds of straight horns MNP arise, some pointed, others blunt, as in cattle when they first begin to grow horns. But the ellipse gives OQ, the shape of an olive or plum, like number V of Schema XI above. \[9. 10. 11.\]

Now, leaving the axis CI, let its perpendicular in the same plane take its place, and let it first be outside the figure, as TX. The figures turned about TX, then, create regular ring-shaped solids, those of the parabola and hyperbola infinite outwards, with the arms CP, CQ turned outwards and gaping, carried round in a circle. But the ellipse, turned about such an outer perpendicular TX to the axis CR, at the distance RY of the centre, Y staying fixed, creates a lying or sitting ring, like the flattened pad on which those who carry buckets set them on their heads. \[12. 13. 14.\] Again imagine this figure in Schema XI above at number I, but with lying ellipses, their vertices turned towards each other, in place of the circular sections. Next let this perpendicular to the axis touch the figure at the vertex C, and let it be CS; and when the figures are turned about it, three kinds of regular close rings will be generated, two infinite outwards, as before, and one finite, from the ellipse GI, the point C staying fixed; and it is lying, sitting or flattened, as before, to be imagined in Schema XI above at number II, if in place of the circular sections there were ellipses touching each other at their vertices. \[15. 16. 17.\] Finally let this perpendicular to the axis cut the figure, and let it be ON: it will cut the plane of the figure into two parts, in the parabola and hyperbola always unequal, since one part PONQ is infinite and ONC finite, so that the conic lines become three segments, two infinite and one finite; but in the ellipse, although both are finite, one is mostly greater, NIO, the other less, OCN. When these six segments of the figures are turned about ON, then, as many new kinds will arise: two hollowed on both sides about the middle (namely at O and N), infinite outwards all round, coming from the infinite parts PONQ turned about ON, and one, from the greater segment of the ellipse, lens-shaped, with the form of a navel above and below. A kind of small sitting melon that is eaten whole grows in such a shape; some mushrooms too grow in this shape on top. \[18. 19. 20.\] Imagine this in Schema XI above at number III, at the figure of the apple, but understand, in place of the joined circles of the section, joined ellipses with the same axis, as in the present Schema XII. Finally, from the lesser parts ONC of the figures come the three remaining solid figures, alike to look at but in fact different; you might not inaptly name the elliptical one OCNL from the fat plum, and for distinction's sake the parabolic and hyperbolic OCNI from spindles. \[21. 22. 23.\] And these two, especially the one []{.page .unprinted n=37}from a very obtuse-angled hyperbola, are particularly notable: for they make figures with a vertex at each end and a circular belly of conspicuous roundness, the rest of the body coming nearer to the straightness of cones towards the vertices on both sides. Among these we shall have to seek the true shape of the barrel, with the vertices O, N cut off.

It was said that in this form of section the segments of the ellipse are not always unequal. When, then, this perpendicular to the axis passes through the centre of the figure R, and is called the minor axis or upright and shorter diameter, then half the figure, turned about this ERK, so that, E staying fixed, the vertex C is carried through I, creates the other kind, the broad spheroid, whose vertices are at E and K and widest circle CRI, on which Archimedes also wrote. \[24.\]

Thus far it would have sufficed, for wine barrels, to list and distinguish the solid figures. But since I said beforehand that in this inquiry I would look a little further beyond the bounds of this little book, I shall add, for kinship's sake, the remaining kinds of solid too.

In the present Schema XIII, then, let any other diameter OS take the place of the axis CI, and let the axis of generation be set in it. Here the ellipse is cut through the centre R into two similar halves, either of which, turned about the fixed OR, creates the shape of a pear, which by right of kinship asks to be joined to the apples, quinces and plums, so that this dessert may be complete. \[25.\] But the other two figures are cut into parts unlike each other, both infinite; and in the obtuse-angled hyperbola there are two cases: for either the obtuse angle is cut by the chosen diameter into two acute parts, or only one part is acute and the other obtuse or right. The parts of the figures, then, except in the last case, when turned, create figures that to the eye do not differ at all from the craters and horns marked with numbers 6, 7, 9 and 10, though in fact they are different, because of the turning of the axis CI, here conical, there cylindrical, and the obliquity here of the whole generating part. \[26. 27. 28. 29.\]

![](/img/stereometria/p37-1.webp){alt="Schema XIII: a parabola, a hyperbola and an ellipse, each with a diameter OS other than its axis and lines parallel to it, beside a flower and leaves"}

But in the obtuse section of the obtuse angle of the hyperbola, the greater part of the figure, turned about such a diameter, adds one kind, or rather not a kind. For it is infinite both downwards and outwards (unless, as in all such, you set an end to it by a conical or cylindrical surface, which arises from the drawing of a straight line), and it lacks a vertex; in its place it is pressed down in the middle into a navel, and around it rises gently upwards, and that without end. \[30.\]

In place of the diameter OS let BD, parallel to it, be taken, which in the ellipse needs no separate consideration from the parallel to the tangent, since to every diameter corresponds its own parallel tangent to the figure. It remains to apply this parallel to the other two figures; let it be, then, either outside the figure or inside. []{.page .unprinted n=38}Outside it cannot be in the parabola and hyperbola, since all parallels to the diameters enter the figure when continued. Let some parallel to the diameter OS be drawn, then, inside these two figures. But again in the parabola a line parallel to a diameter is itself also a diameter; nothing new arises from it. In the hyperbola alone this case has a place and separate consideration: since the diameter OS has cut it into two unlike parts, this parallel to OS is drawn either in its lesser part or in its greater. Let it be drawn in the lesser, and let it be BD; it too, then, cuts the figure into parts yet more unlike, of which the greater creates something like the craters of number 7, or like the infinite one of number 30, and the lesser something like the horns of number 10. \[31. 32. 33.\] Now let some parallel to the diameter OS be drawn in the greater part OQ; which in acute-angled hyperbolas has five cases. For it is drawn either between the dividing diameter and the primary vertex, as between C and O; or through the primary vertex C itself; or between the two vertices, the primary C and that of position A; or beyond the vertex of position A. But in obtuse-angled hyperbolas there is no vertex of position. In any one case the figure is cut into two unlike parts. Thirteen kinds therefore arise, of which four are like the craters of number 7, three like the hollow figure of number 30, four like the horns of number 10, being pointed; the remaining two are rounded at the vertex, like the hyperbolic conoid, number 2: the one from the greater part of a hyperbola, either acute-angled or, if obtuse-angled, divided by a suitable diameter, as was said (for it must cut the obtuse angle of the hyperbola into two acute parts), is like the obtuse-angled conoid, and the one from the lesser part like the acute-angled conoid. \[34.–46.\]

Let the last line be a tangent to the section, but away from the primary vertex C, as EF. If the parabola and hyperbola are turned about it, they generate two infinite kinds, like those of numbers 15 and 16, but less regular, since they extend infinitely sideways. \[47. 48.\] But the ellipse turned about this EF generates a close ring leaning inwards, related to number 17, which you will imagine in Schema XI of the five kinds of turning at number II, but with ellipses, equally inclined towards their mutual contact, in place of the circular sections. \[49.\] The asymptote could be counted among the tangents, for it is as it were a tangent to the section at an infinitely distant point; and here a new kind is to be considered, as it were midway between open and close rings. \[50.\]

In place of the tangent let its parallel be taken, and let it first be outside the figure, and let the figures be turned about it as an axis; four kinds of solid rings result: from the parabola and hyperbola rings infinite sideways, whose back in the parabolic one always stands out in a circle, while among the hyperbolic some have a prominent back, and others become higher outwards than where they are narrowest, not very unlike the figure of number 13. \[51. 52. 53.\] But from the ellipse turned about GH comes a finite ring leaning inwards, wider on one side, very nearly in the shape of a tiara or Turkish turban, if you take away its peak. \[54.\] Imagine this too at the first form of turning, but with ellipses, equally inclined to each other, in place of the circular sections.

[]{.page .unprinted n=39}If the parallel to the tangent is inside the figure, as LK, cutting it into two parts, many kinds result, because there are also many cases. For either LK does not cut the axis inside the figure, or it cuts it. And if it cuts it, then either it passes through the primary vertex C, or it takes it in, shutting out the vertex of position about O, which follows from setting the figure upright along EF, if there is one at all (for in the obtuse-angled hyperbola, if LK or FE makes an obtuse angle towards the figure with the opposite asymptote RB, there is no vertex); or it passes through this vertex of position; or it also takes this in, in the part cut off. I have listed five cases, and all have place in all three figures; so fifteen sections, and two segments of the figure in each. But in the hyperbola the three greater segments of the first three cases are double as regards their effect; for the infinite part runs out there either downwards, as in the parabola, or upwards. There are therefore thirty-three kinds of solid from here, of which thirteen are infinite sideways, and of these nine are hollowed into a navel on both sides (six of them with a prominent circular lip, like the craters of number 7, but differing in this, that they are like a mountain hollowed underneath as well; three without a prominent lip, whose height is not finite, like the figure of number 19), two pointed, horns as in number 10, and two with a well-rounded vertex, conoids as in number 2, namely when the line about which the figure is to be turned passes through the vertex of position, where there is one. \[55.–67.\] Of these thirteen, five are parabolic and eight hyperbolic. The remaining twenty are finite, and thicker on one side and thinner on the other. For of them fifteen kinds (five from each figure) end in a point: nine pointed at both ends, three from each figure, which we may call kernels; three, one from each figure, well rounded at the thicker end and so akin to the broad spheroid, which we can compare to strawberries or pine nuts; and finally three, again one from each figure, hollow at the thicker end and pointed at the thinner, like the husk of the bladder cherry, which the Germans call Jewish cherries, secretly signifying the glans of the penis with its foreskin. \[68. 69. 80. 71. 72. 73. 74. 75. 76. 77. 88. 79. 80. 81. 82.\] The last five are kinds of pear, generated from the greater part of the ellipse in the five said cases of this section, all hollow, three at both ends and two at one only; at the other, thin end, one is rounded and like a spheroid, and one pointed, almost the same as the bladder cherry, born from the lesser part of the ellipse. \[83. 84. 85. 86. 87.\] The sum is eighty-seven; and with the five figures from the circle added, as the heads of the families, they make ninety-two forms.

So many, then, are the kinds of solid figure that result from conic sections turned in a circle, without now counting separately the segments of each, or figures put together from segments. For example, a pewter dish mostly consists of three surfaces: at the bottom a segment of a sphere, round about a parabolic crater, while the rim is from the surface of a very obtuse cone, or else an oblique zone of a globe. And although the skilful measurements of several of them come to the same thing, the geometer must not be ignorant of such manifold differences of generation, lest, when unwary, he be drawn into inextricable snares in considering generally some that are special. Let geometers, then, exercise themselves on each of these, after the example of Archimedes, who of these considered four only, and a fifth, the globe itself: []{.page .unprinted n=40}not because they were the most useful or most common (for what has the parabolic conoid over the ring, the apple, the pear, the plum, the kernel?) but because they were the simplest, and nearest to the globe, and lent themselves to his genius. For the present we shall consider only those by which access lies open to the hyperbolic spindles, whose frustums are our barrels; so I shall add the following theorems about them.

### Theorem XVIII.

Every ring of circular or elliptical section is equal to a cylinder whose height equals the length of the circumference described by the centre of the figure turned, and whose base is the same as the section of the ring.

I mean the section made by a plane drawn through the centre of the space within the ring, at right angles to the ring's surface. The proof of this theorem is plain in part from Theorem XVI, and can be set up from the same elements by which Archimedes laid down the principles of solid geometry.

For if the ring GCD in Schema XI, but whole, is cut from the centre A of its space into infinitely many smallest slices ED, each of them will be thinner towards the centre A by as much as a part of it, such as E, is nearer the centre A than F is, and the line through F perpendicular to ED in the cutting plane; and thicker by as much towards the outside D: and the said extremes, D and S, taken together, give twice the thickness that is in the middle of the slices.

This reasoning would not hold if the parts of the slices E, D on this side and that of the circumference FG and of the lines perpendicular through F and G were not equal and equally placed.

### Corollary I.

This way of measuring holds both for the circular form of ring and for the elliptical, tall, sitting or leaning; both for open rings and for close ones: indeed, in all rings, whatever figure results from their upright section in place of the circle ED, provided that in the plane through AD at right angles to the ring the parts of the section on this side and that of F are equal and equally placed on either side. We shall test this on a square section. Let the ring be square in body, and let a square be understood under ED. This ring has another way of measurement as well. For it is the outer part of a cylinder whose base is the circle with radius AD and height DE: from this cylinder, by Theorem XVI, the core must be taken, that is, the cylinder whose base is the circle with radius AE and height ED. So what is made by multiplying ED by the circle AD less the circle AE equals the body of the ring created by the plane square. And if ED were multiplied by the square of AD less the square of AE, the ratio of the body made to the body of a quarter of the ring would be as the square to the circle, namely 14 to 11. Let AE be 1. Then AD is 4, whose square is 16; but the square of AE is 4. So the difference of the squares is 12, which multiply by the height 2: the body is 24, whose []{.page .unprinted n=41}four times is 96. And as 14 to 11, so 96 to 75 and three sevenths, the body of the square ring. This is by the rule of Theorem XVI. But by the present way, since AF is 3 and FG 6, and as 7 to 22, so 6 to 19 less one seventh: this will be the length of the circumference FG, for the height of the cylinder. And since ED is 2 and its square 4, for the base of the cylinder, multiply 4 by 19 less one seventh: the result is 76 less four sevenths. See, the theorem is true this way too.

### Theorem XIX and Analogy.

A close ring is equal to a cylinder whose base is the circle of the ring's section and whose height is equal to the length of that circle.

For this way holds in absolutely every ratio of AE to AF, and holds still in the close ring, where the centre F of the circle EC being turned describes the circle FG, equal to DA turned. For such a close ring is cut from A into slices which at A have no thickness, at D twice the thickness at F, just as the circle through D is twice the circle through F.

### Corollary.

The cylindrical body made (in Schema XI) by turning the mixed quadrilateral MIKN is, by force of the same proof, equal to a column that has this quadrilateral for its base and the length of the circle through FG for its height. But the outer rim IKD, surrounding the cylindrical body on the outside as a hoop does a barrel, has nothing at all to do with this theorem, but must be investigated by other principles.

### Analogy.

Again, this way holds through all the cylindrical bodies or segments of the apple (as also of the quince), thinner and thinner, until finally IK and MN coincide, which happens in the generation of the globe, number IV, where in place of the two lines MN and IK there is the single BC; so that in that body the proof and use of this theorem first wholly cease.

### Corollary.

A globe is to the close ring created by the same circle as 7 to 33. For a third of the radius multiplied by four times the great circle, or two thirds of the diameter multiplied by the area of the great circle, create a cylinder equal to the globe. But the cylinder equal to the close ring has the same base, and for its height the circumference. So as the circumference is to two thirds of the diameter, 33 to 7, so is the close ring to the globe.

### []{.page .unprinted n=42}Theorem XX.

The zone of an apple is made up of a zone of a globe and an upright segment of a cylinder, the base of which segment is the segment that is missing in the figure that generates the apple, and its height is equal to the circle described by the centre of the greater segment.

![](/img/stereometria/p42-1.webp){alt="Schema XIV: a tall rectangle MNDS standing for the apple's body opened out into a cylinder, with the diagonal AS, the segment MDN at the foot, and shaded elliptical slices"}

Proof. Let the body of the apple be laid out into a cylindrical segment by the same laws by which Archimedes, Theorem II, laid out the area of the circle into a right-angled triangle; and let AD be the radius of the great circle in the body of the apple, from whose point D let DS be raised, the length of that great circle stretched out straight, to be conceived in a cylindrical surface. For the line MN is as it were a common edge at which all the circular solid segments end. And when the circumference of the great circle is stretched out straight into DS, those circular solid segments are stretched out with it, and become the elliptical MSN, except the first, MDN. But the force of this transformation will shine out more clearly in what follows. Let the area MDN be cut by lines parallel to MN into a number of smallest segments of equal breadth, as it were lines, and let the points A and S be joined, and onto the line AS, from the points of the diameter AD made by the sections of the area, let the perpendiculars FG, OL be drawn. Let F be the centre, and let the perpendicular from F cut AS at G, and through G let GT be drawn parallel to FD. Finally let O be the midpoint of the section IK, and from it the perpendicular OL, cutting AS at L, and through L let LR be drawn parallel to OD. When, then, the figure is turned about MN, the little area at MN creates almost nothing, since it moves least; but its parallel through F now moves in a circle of length FG, the line through O in a circle of length OL, and so all of them; and the parts of the cylindrical body marked by FG, OL are equal to those cylindrical coats, as it were, in the globe which the lines generate in turning the figure MDN about MN, by Theorem XVI. The whole figure, then, namely the prism of the cylinder MNDS, made up of the bodies of all the coats stretched out straight, is equal to the whole body of the apple, made up of the coats.

Further, the cylindrical body on the base IMNK up to L, the cylinder being cut by the plane in which the lines OL and KI lie, will be equal to the cylindrical body of the apple with the outer zone taken away. ### []{.page .unprinted n=51}On Spindles.

Thus far the cylinder and the globe, or in its place the spheroid, transformed into a segment of their cylinder, have come to our aid in giving the measures of apples, lemons, quinces, sitting melons, olives and elliptical plums. For when we could not find the laws of the whole bodies in the figures themselves, we found the measures of the parts of the body in the parts of cylinders. And since certain parts of a cylinder have a definite definition, but no knowledge or laws of bulk in themselves, or none yet brought to light, the globe and the spheroid came in, which, having their measure of bulk beforehand, when transformed into such a part of a cylinder brought the same laws of bulk into it. There remains now the more difficult consideration of parabolic and hyperbolic spindles, in which the method of proof used so far fails us again. For even if you transform a spindle in the same way as the lemon, the olive and the plum into a prism of a column that keeps the curvature of the conic line of the sections OCH, PCQ or MCN (in Schema X) along its whole upright back, the body of this figure can be proved no more than that of the spindle itself. For first, there is no whole here to which the prism can be compared, since the column, like the section itself, will be infinite towards the side MN or PQ; and second, neither the globe with its great circle nor the spheroid fits into such a conic column: for either the circle touches the conic line on the inside at a single point, if it is described from the focus A of the figure through its vertex C; or, if a slightly larger circle is described through C, it does touch the figures outside at C but at once cuts them at two points next to C, and otherwise wholly parts from the section.

It remains, then, that just as for bodies created from a segment of a circle we took refuge in the globe, and for elliptical ones in the spheroid, so for those from the parabola and hyperbola we take refuge in the conoids of the same kind; and if the attempt does not wholly succeed, we shall call on geometers to help with the rest.

### Theorem XXIII.

Two cones created from a scalene right-angled triangle, the one with the lesser, the other with the greater of the sides about the right angle set up as axis, are in the ratio of the sides that describe the bases of the cones.

![](/img/stereometria/p51-1.webp){alt="Schema XVI: two lines crossing at right angles at A, with B above, D below, C to the left and E to the right, joined by the lines CB, CD and BE"}

Let ABC be a right-angled triangle, of whose sides about the right angle the lesser, BA, is made the axis; and when the figure is turned, let the hypotenuse BC create the conical surface CBE, whose vertex is B and base CE. Again let the greater side AC be made the axis, and let the figure turned about the fixed AC create the cone BCD, with vertex C and the circle BD as base. I say that as BA is to AC, so is the body contained by the circle CE and the conical surface CEB to the body of the cone BDC.

[]{.page .unprinted n=52}Proof. By what was cited in Theorem XVII, the ratio of the cone EBC to the cone BCD is compounded of the ratio of the circle EC to the circle BD and of the ratio of the height AB to the height AC; but the ratio of the circle EC to the circle BD is the square of the ratio of the radius AC to the radius AB. So the ratio of the cones EBC to BCD is compounded of the ratio of AC to AB, again of the same AC to AB, and third of AB to AC. But the ratio of AC to AB, compounded with the ratio of AB to AC, makes the ratio of equality, which is the least of ratios, that is, the limit, and an equality, which added to or taken from the other ratios changes nothing. So of the three elements of the ratio of the cones, the last two of which cancel each other, only the first remains, and the cone EBC is to the cone BCD as AC, the radius of the circle EC, to AB, the radius of the circle BD.

### Theorem XXIV.

A long spheroid inscribed in a broad spheroid, so that they have the same diameters but their axes exchanged, is to the broad spheroid as the shorter diameter to the longer.

In Schema XII let there be the long spheroid CEI on the right, whose vertices are C, I, and the broad one KCE on the left, with vertices K, E; and let the axis CI of the former be equal to the diameter CI of the latter, and the diameter KE of the former equal to the axis KE of the latter. I say that the long ellipsoid, or egg, on the right is to the broad one, or lens, on the left as KE, the shorter diameter, to CI, the longer. For let cones be described, in the half egg KCE, whose vertex is C and base the circle KE, and in the half lens CKI, whose vertex is K and base the circle CI. By the previous theorem, then, as KR, the radius of the circle EK, is to RC, the radius of the circle CI, so is the cone KCE to the cone IKC. But half a spheroid is always twice the cone inscribed in it on the same axis and the same circular base. So the halves of the spheroids too, and so the whole spheroids, are in the ratio of KR to RC, that is, of their doubles KD to CI.

### Theorem XXV.

A segment of a globe seems to have to the lemon described by the same segment of a circle the ratio that the radius of the base of the segment has to the axis or height of the segment.

Let others seek a lawful proof: what I cannot prove apodictically I shall make good dictically, using four pieces of evidence. The first is from analogy. For what is true in the half globe, as in the greatest segment, which is the beginning of the segments, as also in the half spheroid, and likewise in the least segment, and as it were in the last []{.page .unprinted n=53}limit of all segments, seems to have place also in the intermediate segments. And in the half globe the case is this: just as the sides of a quadrant about the right angle have the ratio of equality to each other, so what is created by turning the quadrant about the perpendicular is equal to what is created by turning the same quadrant about the base. And in the smallest ones this ratio likewise has place, because the smaller the segment of the globe and the lemon in it, the less the cones inscribed in the figures differ from them; and those cones have the said ratio, as Theorem XXIII has it; so the solids about them have it too. Though I admit that the inference from what is absolutely least to what is next to the least is not everywhere safe.

Second, the said ratio has place in the half spheroid, even though there the ratio of equality between the diameters does not reign, as in the globe; and indeed in infinitely many spheroids, and infinitely many ratios of diameters through them, as in the previous theorem. But just as the long spheroid is inscribed in the broad spheroid, so too the whole lemon is inscribed in the doubled segment of the hemisphere, and the generation is understood as similar in both; so, since the said ratio holds between the long and the broad spheroid, it will also hold, as it seems, between the whole lemon and the doubled segment of the half globe. Third, this stands in the place of a full proof: that in Schema XVIII following, the oblique segment of the circle contained under the straight line AE and the arc AE, which creates the excess both of the segment and of the half lemon over their cones, is of equal breadth above at A and below at E; so that the parts at E turned about PA make a motion in the same ratio to the motion of the parts at A turned about PE as PE is to PA. The body of the oblique zones, then, is heaped onto the inscribed cones in the same ratio as the cones themselves are to each other. Fourth, to these is added calculation and the testimony of numbers, which, though handled most laboriously and finely, dividing the diameter into 1 00000 particles, yet in the end prove unfit to refute this ratio.

In the example above PE was nine times PB; so let the segment HEA of the globe also be nine times the half lemon described by AEP about the fixed PE. Let the segment HEA be sought. AL was 22, LK or PE 27. So the squares are 484 and 729. And since the areas of circles are to each other as the squares of their radii, and the circle AC above had 10050 23661, there will come to the area HPE, which is the base of the proposed segment, 15137 64977 parts.

And since PA was 3, and the remainder to the diameter 243, and the radius 123: as 243 to 123, or in its place to 100000, so 3 to 1234½, the increase in the height of the segment for the height of the equal cone, and that in the measure used in the canon; and since the height of the segment PA in this measure is 2439, the height of the equal cone is 3673½, a third of which, 1224½, multiplied by the area of the base found above, creates the body of the segment, 185 28483 31848, the ninth part of which is 20 58720 36872. But above, the body of the whole lemon was 36 etc., and so its half is 18 24638 77472, which is indeed less than a ninth part of the segment, less even than a tenth of it; but this is in an untrustworthy handling of numbers about very small things. For it concerns the quantity of a body less than a twenty-thousandth of the globe. And the difference in this little body (much greater than the one we are disputing) arises from a single hundred-thousandth part of the radius: because the sine of the arc AE, that is, PE, was taken as 21951, which was a little too great, though less than 21952. And if you take 21952 and carry through the process with it, the body of the lemon will come out 42 47320 62579, whose half, 21 etc., is already greater than a ninth part of the segment found here, because 21952 too is greater than the true value. By these []{.page .unprinted n=54}numbers, then, nothing can be brought against the ratio of the half lemon to its segment of the globe expressed in the theorem.

### Theorem XXVI.

If a straight line touches a conic section, and the segment of a spheroid or the conoid generated by it, at the circumference of the base, meeting the axis; and if the lines, turned about the fixed diameter of the base, create solids, the tangent a cone and the conic sections a plum, olive or spindle, each of its own kind; and if the same tangent, turned about the fixed axis, creates another cone: then the ratio of the half plum or olive to the segment of the spheroid, and of the spindle to its conoid, will be very nearly equal to the ratio of the first cone to the second.

In Schema XII let there be the conic section OCN, a parabola above, a hyperbola in the middle and an ellipse below, whose axis is CI; and by turning half the section OCN, namely CN, about the fixed ON, so that the point N of the section stays fixed and C passes through I, let the half body CIN of the plum, olive or spindle OCNI be understood to be created. By turning the same half CN of the section about the fixed CI, so that, C staying fixed, N passes through O, let the segment of the spheroid or the conoid OCN be understood to be created, with vertex C and the circle ON as base. I say that if some line touches the section or solid at the ends N or O, two cones are created by that line, by the same turnings as just described, very nearly such that in those cones lies the ratio of the half body of the plum, olive or spindle CIN to the segment of the spheroid or conoid OCN. Thus far we needed Schema XII to explain the theorem. Now the rest will be taken from Schema XVIII.

For the theorem concerns the segment of the spheroid and the two conoids, parabolic and hyperbolic; and it has potentially three parts in it. The first and second are certain: that this ratio is less than that of the previous theorem; and second, that some ratio is proved greater; the third is not yet quite certain, that the ratio is exactly the one expressed in this theorem. And since everything is clearer in the hyperbolic case, in Schema XVIII let there be the conic section called the hyperbola, FCG, the line marked with dots; and an arc of a circle is also described through FCG. The hyperbola, then, cuts it at F; from there the circle goes on towards S and the hyperbola towards R, always inside the circle, until at the vertex C it touches the circle on the inside, as is proved in Apollonius, *Conics* IV, Props. XXV, XXVI. Let a conoid be made from FCG, whose vertex is C, axis VCO, base the circle FG, and its radius FO; and let V be the centre of the figure, and VX, VZ the asymptotes, whose intersections with FG extended are X and Z. And let the straight line EY touch the figure at F, on the circumference of the base, meeting the axis between the centre V []{.page .unprinted n=55}and the vertex C; let it meet it at Y. And let the triangle FCG be inscribed in the figure on the same base FG. So, whereas before the body of the half lemon described by the arc of the circle FSC about the fixed FO had to the body of the spherical segment FCG, described by the same arc FSC but turned about the fixed CO, the ratio of CO to OF: now this theorem asserts of the half spindle described by half the hyperbola, FRC, about the fixed FO, and of the conoid described by the same FRC but about the fixed CO, another ratio, namely that of YO to OF; for this is the ratio of the cone described by the tangent FY about FO to the cone described by the same FY about YO. And it is plain that the ratio of YO to OF is less, that is, nearer to equality, than that of CO to OF; and since VX and YF meet towards X, the ratio of VO to OX is again less than that of YO to OF.

Just as YO to OF, then, is midway in quantity between CO to OF and VO to OX, so it can be proved that the body of the half spindle is to the body of the conoid in a ratio midway in quantity between CO to OF and VO to OX.

Let it be proved first of CO to OF; and the proof will hold of the parabolic conoid too. It is plain, then, that the spindle of the line FRC is less than the lemon of the arc FSC, and likewise the conoid FRCG less than the spherical segment FSCG. And since the plane figure contained between the arc of the circle FSC and the hyperbola FRC is of unequal breadth at the parts F and C, being wider about F, where the lines cut each other, and narrower about C, where they touch each other: the coat that the segment of the globe has put round the conoid is therefore thicker towards the base FG and thinner towards the vertex C. Conversely, the coat that the lemon has put round the spindle is thinner towards its base at C than towards its vertex F. The conoid and the spindle do not, then, lose proportionally, but the conoid loses more and the spindle less. For although about the vertex, in turn, the conoid loses less than the spindle about its vertex F, there is still no full compensation, because the motion of the parts at the vertex ends within a short space, while the motion of the parts about the base spreads into a wider circuit. The spindle, then, is nearer to the conoid than the lemon to the segment of the sphere, or CO (by the previous theorem) to OF; just as YO too is nearer to OF than CO to OF.

Here we have used the previous theorem, which has as yet no lawful proof. But the same method holds even if we put the cones FCG in place of the segment FSCG and the lemon. For the cone made by the line CF about FO has to the cone made by the same line FC about CO the ratio of CO to OF. And the plane figure contained by the hyperbola FRC and the straight line FC, which creates the excess of the conoid and the spindle over their cones, is wider towards C, because the hyperbola is more curved there, and narrower towards F, where the hyperbola gradually degenerates into a straight line. So again, proportional amounts are not added to these cones; for more is added to the cone of the spindle to make the spindle, in its ratio, than to the cone of the conoid to make the conoid; so the body of the spindle is greater, relative to the conoid, than CO relative to OF; that is, the ratio of the lesser (the spindle) to the greater (the conoid) is less and nearer to equality.

[]{.page .unprinted n=56}Now it must also be proved of VO to OX that this ratio is less than the ratio of the half spindle to the conoid. And the proof is peculiar to the hyperbola, since the parabola lacks asymptotes. Again, then, as before, the figure contained by the three straight lines FX, XV, VC and the hyperbola CRF is wider towards V than towards X: so the body or matrix in which the conoid lies hidden is thicker at the vertex V than at the base XZ; in turn, the matrix in which the spindle lies hidden is thinner at its vertex FX than at its base about VC. So more is added to the cone whose axis is XV, in its ratio, than to the cone whose axis is VO, in its. That cone, then, is greater relative to this one than the spindle relative to OX, and so the ratio of VO to OX is less and nearer to equality than that of the half spindle to the conoid.

But since between the ratios CO to OF and VO to CX there are infinitely many other intermediate ratios, not only the one ratio YO to OF, the inference is not necessary, but at most probable, an affirmative conclusion in the third figure of argument from mere particulars.

### Analogy.

Consider whether, while in the segments of the globe the ratio of the inscribed cones always holds, in those of the spheroid the cones that have the true ratio of the solids (here, say, of the plum to the segment of the broad spheroid, or of the olive to that of the long one) push out, one its vertex and the other the end of its base, beyond the vertex of the ellipse, but inside the tangent; in the parabolic conoid this very tangent creates the cones of the ratio sought, so that the height of one cone is exactly twice the height of the segment of the conoid; and finally in the hyperbolic conoid the vertex and base of these cones run out beyond the tangent towards the centre of the figure. The force of this analogy is great, and almost demonstrative.

Yet it is not enough to have proved this; the very points must also be shown, between the tangents and the vertex of the ellipse or the centre of the hyperbola.

### Theorem XXVII.

If one of the sides about the right angle of any right-angled triangle is cut both into two equal parts and in the ratio of the other sides; and if various conic sections meet at the opposite angle, touching one another and the side opposite the right angle, and having their primary vertices on the side that was cut: those from the top to the midpoint will all be hyperbolas; the one falling on the midpoint itself a parabola; those from there to the proportional section upright ellipses of every kind; the one falling on the []{.page .unprinted n=57}proportional section itself a circle; and finally those from there to the right angle will be transverse ellipses of every kind, in which the vertex is spoken of improperly, for the end of the shorter axis.

![](/img/stereometria/p57-1.webp){alt="Schema XVII: the right-angled triangle CAB, its side CA extended down to D, with conic sections drawn from B to the points V, O, I, N and E on CA"}

Let BAC be a right-angled triangle, whose side AC is cut into equal parts at O; and let the angle CBA be bisected by the line BN, so that as AB is to BC, so is AN to NC. AN will therefore be shorter than AO. Then let there be a point V between C and O, a point I between O and N, and a point E between N and A; and let various conic sections touch each other and the straight line BC at the point B, whose vertices are, in this order, V, O, I, N, E. I say that BV are hyperbolas, BO a parabola, BI upright ellipses, BN a circle, BE transverse ellipses.

First of BO. Since the straight line BC touches the conic section BO, whose axis or diameter is CA and vertex O, at B, meeting the diameter outside the section at C, and BA is drawn ordinatewise from the point of contact B to the diameter, being perpendicular to the axis CA; and since CO is equal to OA: BO will be a parabola, by the converse of Apollonius I, 37.

Second of BV. The rest remaining, since V is the vertex and CV less than half of CA, let twice CV be taken from CA, and let it be made, as this remainder to CV, so CV to CF, outwards. Since, then, what is made from CF and the said remainder equals the square of CV, let common quantities be added to both, the square of CF and two rectangles VCF: so that on one side the rectangle CFA is put together, on the other the square of FV. And since these are equal, the section BV will be a hyperbola, by the converse of Apollonius I, 37, whose centre is F.

Third of BI, BN and BE. The foregoing remaining, since I, N, E are vertices and IA, NA, EA less than half of CA, let twice IA etc. be taken from CA, and let it be made, as these remainders to IA etc., so these to AD, inwards. For the rest it will be proved by the same method as before that the squares of FI etc. are equal to the rectangles CDA; and therefore by the same converse of Apollonius the conic sections BI, BN, BE will be finite, with their centres D inside the figures, and so ellipses or a circle.

Fourth of BN, presupposing what was just proved of it in the third place. Since moreover AN is to NC as AB to BC, which is a unique ratio in any one triangle, while hyperbolas and ellipses have various ratios, and the parabola indeed a single one, but the ratio of equality: BN cannot be any other of the conic sections than a circle. And so indeed it happens in the circle. For let BN be a circle, and F its centre, which is []{.page .unprinted n=58}joined to the point of contact B; then CBD will be a right angle; but CAB too was a right angle; so as CA to DB, so is DB, that is, DN, to DA. But DN is to DA as CB to BA, and as CN to NA. The arc of the circle, then, cuts the side on which, extended, it has its centre, in the ratio of the sides AB, BC.

### Corollary and Analogy.

In this triangle, then, there can be infinitely many hyperbolas, of which the most obtuse, to speak by analogy, is BC, whose vertex V and centre F coincide at C itself, the angle of the asymptotes, and the section itself degenerates entirely into two straight lines (the cone, that is, being cut through its vertex); and the most acute of the hyperbolas in this triangle is, by analogy, the parabola itself, whose vertex is at the midpoint O and centre F at an infinite distance. And if CV is a third part of CA, CF and CV will be equal; if CV is less, it exceeds CV; if greater, it exceeds CF.

So the upright ellipses BI pass between O and N, infinitely many, of which the most acute at the vertex is, by analogy, the parabola BO itself, whose vertex is O and centre D at an infinite distance, and the most obtuse of these ellipses at the vertex is the circle BN. From this the transverse ellipses begin, again infinitely many, of which the most acute about the vertex (improperly so called, since it is the belly) is the circle ON itself, and from it the ever more obtuse BE, until at last they vanish into the mere straight line BA, having its improper vertex E and centre D at the point A itself, but its vertex properly so called at B, and most obtuse about A, being merely straight. And if AE is a third part of CA, EA and AD will be equal; if EA is less, it exceeds AD; if greater, it is exceeded by it.

### Corollary II.

From this, namely from the tangents, it is easy to judge the kind of a cut solid.[^p58a] For if two straight lines FY, GY, touching the cut solid at points of the circumferences of the cutting circles, in Schema XVIII at G and F, make their mutual intersection Y such that YC is equal to CO, half the difference of the circles, the middle and the cutting one: it will be the frustum of a parabolic spindle; if CY is less, it will be the frustum of a hyperbolic spindle; if greater, of the kind first of plums, then of lemons (if YF is to FO as YC to CO), and finally of elliptical olives, as when CY is twice CO.

### Theorem XXVIII.

If the four kinds of conic section, the circle, ellipses, the parabola and hyperbolas, touch one another at a common vertex, and besides meet at two other points equally distant from the vertex, all are cut by all at those two []{.page .unprinted n=59}points, and the circumference of the circle within the intersections is outermost and contains the elliptical ones, these the parabolic; innermost are the hyperbolic, and of these the more obtuse are further in, and nearer to their asymptotes.

For since the sections are supposed to be of different kinds, and dissimilar even within one kind, they cannot have the same parts; but they will either touch each other at a single point, or cut each other, and the arcs between the points will lie apart from each other, wholly from wholly, by Apollonius IV, 24. And since they are all supposed to touch each other at the vertex of the cone, and also to meet at two other points, none of them, then, will meet any of the others at more points, even if the parabola and hyperbolas are continued to infinity, by Apollonius IV, 26. And since they are supposed to meet at three points, they will not touch each other at two of these points; for if they touched at two, they would not meet at the third, by Apollonius IV, 27. It follows, then, that the two remaining meetings are intersections; for every meeting is either a contact or an intersection. And at intersections the order is reversed.

And since the three points are supposed on the circumference of a circle, a single circle will pass through them, by what is proved in Euclid, Book III. Likewise the parabola will be single. For suppose there are different ones; since it is supposed that they touch at the vertex of the cone, if they are different and cut each other, they will also have different tangents at their common intersection; so by the same method of proof that Apollonius IV, 28 uses in proving that two parabolas do not touch at more than one point, the matter will come down to an impossibility, and the whole will become equal to the part. So there are not different parabolas, but one only, passing through the three points.

And since hyperbolas, the more obtuse they are, run out the further outside beyond the intersections, as is plain of itself, within the intersections they must be by so much further in. And since among similar hyperbolas, that is, those made with the same angle of asymptotes, the one formed with greater sides is held to be greater, the obtuse ones here are smaller in their kind than the more acute in theirs. On two counts, then, the inner one has nearer asymptotes: both because it is smaller in its kind, and because it is more obtuse than its companion; for the more obtuse, as was said in the previous theorem, come nearer and nearer to their asymptotes and at last coincide with them. Indeed it was also proved in the previous theorem that in one series, that of the more obtuse, the centre is nearer to the tangent than the tangent is to the vertex, and in the other, that of the more acute, further away. And the tangent to the inner one is itself inner too. This can also be proved absolutely by Apollonius I, 37. So since the hyperbolas outside embrace the parabola, within the intersections the parabola in turn will embrace them; for the same reason, since the parabola outside the intersections embraces the ellipses, within the intersections the ellipses will in turn enclose the parabola. Finally, since the circle touching the ellipses at the vertex is supposed to cut them in two places, elliptical crescents will be cut off by the circle and will lie outside the circle; before the intersections, then, the arcs of the ellipses will be within the arc of the circle.

### []{.page .unprinted n=60}Theorem XXIX.

If a lemon, plums, a parabolic spindle, hyperbolic spindles and a doubled cone, all cut off, have the same circles, both the cutting circles and the middle circle of the bodies, the lemon will be the greatest, and the rest in the same order of size of body in which they are listed here.

This is easily proved from the previous theorem. For the lemon is created from a segment of a circle, plums from vertical segments of ellipses, spindles from vertical segments of the parabola and of hyperbolas, and the doubled cone from an isosceles triangle. And since the bodies are supposed to have the same cutting circles, the arcs of all the generating lines will cut each other at the two points through which the cutting circles pass. And since the same great circle in the middle of the body is given to all the bodies, all the generating lines touch each other at a common vertex, which by the turning of each figure creates that circle in the middle of the whole body. And since the conic sections embrace one another in the order given here, the segments too will exceed one another in the same order. The doubled and cut cone (contained in Schema XVIII by the straight lines HAE GCF) will therefore be the least. First the hyperbolas will each put a coat round it, to make cut hyperbolic spindles; the parabola will put on one more, to make the parabolic spindle; then the ellipses will each add one again, to make cut elliptical plums. Finally the circle, with the arc FSQCG, will put on it the last coat and make the cut lemon.

### Theorem XXX. A problem put to geometers.

To find the ratio of the segments of a lemon, olive, plum or spindle made by a plane parallel to the axis.

Its use cannot be obscure; the knowledge is lacking. In laying out the solidity of the lemon straight, that is, into a portion of a cylindrical prism, there corresponds to such a segment of the lemon a segment of that cylindrical portion made by a surface like a cylindrical one, or rather in a way like part of a rolled sheet of paper: for in one direction it is straight, and parallel to a straight line in the base of the cylindrical portion; but upwards it is curved, yet with the curvature neither of a circle, that is certain, nor of a conic section, as far as I know, though among the conic sections it is most like the elliptical, because it bends more at the top. And even if the line of this curvature were known, the solidity of such a portion would not yet be given by it, at least by what has been established so far.
