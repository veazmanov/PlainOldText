## []{.page .unprinted n=A1}Practica. On the Four Seasons, and Other Significations of the Planets and Eclipses.

Set out for the year after the birth of Christ 1598. By M. Johannes Kepler, Mathematician to the Honourable Estates of the Duchy of Styria.

Printed at Graz in Styria by Hans Schmidt.

## []{.page n=A2}To the Well-born, Noble and Strict Lords N. N., the Lords Deputies of the Honourable and Most Praiseworthy Estates of the Duchy of Styria, etc., My Gracious and Commanding LORDS.

Well-born, noble and strict, gracious and commanding LORDS: experience bears witness that nothing has brought God's fine gift, the noble art of the course and working of the heavens, into contempt so much as the claiming of too much for it, and the unseemly, superstitious boasting that has turned learned men away from it. I have therefore undertaken in my Practicas of the last two years to cut back this immoderate glory of astrology, and to show that the yearly prognostications attached to the calendars are in no way to be built upon, but should rather be read for honest amusement, especially by learned, understanding and quiet people: seeing that the art itself has no such foundation from which any particular event could be foretold in specie, other than in general terms. But since I know well that many are misled by the examples of certain excellent astrologers, who have foretold such things not only for a given time (which is not wholly to be denied to an astrologer) but also in specie and with every circumstance, at which people rightly wonder greatly, so that such people []{.page .unprinted n=A2v}still hold that it is not the art but the unskilled artist who causes the uncertainty of the Practicas: I have wished to give a short account of how matters stand with such strange and very exact foretellings. According to the opinion of the learned there are two kinds of prophecy. Some are taken from natural causes known to men; others, and many of them, come about where no man, and for the most part not even the prophet himself, sees or knows any natural ground. And to give some examples of both, but first of the latter: among these the first place belongs to the divine revelations made to the patriarchs, prophets and saints of God in visions or dreams. These have their certainty from God the Lord, their proper author, before whom all nature and all the arts that flow from it should rightly cover their faces, hold their tongues and confess their uncertainty. In the same way the Devil undertakes to reveal certain things to his sorcerers: but in this there is nothing but deceit and no certainty at all, except what he perhaps takes from nature, which would not have been impossible for a man either. Then there are found in every age, as is known from Ptolemy and from daily experience, certain deranged people who often, with thoughtless spirit and in the midst of their folly, foretell wonderful things which afterwards turn out to be so in fact. To these are like all who are afflicted with diseases of the head, or who are at the point of death: to whom strange and certain things often occur, of which no one knows the cause, except that we suppose that God himself chooses what is foolish before this world to proclaim such great things through them, and that his strength is made mighty in the weak.

The infallible dreams of certain people, on which an []{.page n=A3}old Greek writer, Artemidorus, wrote a whole book, may also not unfittingly be put here. And, to come to my purpose: it often happens that an astrologer, in a Practica or nativity he has in hand, is not content on some of its points with the well-founded general rules of astrology, but steps outside the bounds on the strength of other, political considerations and sets down something in specie; or else simply follows the most absurd rules that the superstitious Arabs ever managed to invent, and so also arrives ad speciem. Whoever puts many such things in his Practicas cannot well fail: something must turn out right for him once, and mostly in a way other than he himself had thought; so that, if he were asked, he would have to confess that it was not his art, still less nature, but luck alone that so arranged things that he wrote in specie what afterwards came true. For luck is a wonderful master in all such ungrounded things, such as the geomantic fall of the dice, the Alphabet of Aristotle, the Jewish Cabbala, etc., and together with men's superstition seldom leaves the soothsayer in the lurch. But if it happened to an astrologer often, or usually, or even once in a weighty matter, that his ungrounded thoughts came true so exactly in specie, one would have to confess that his thoughts and pen too, like those mentioned above, were governed by a hidden power: as such things are often ordained by God for the sake of the person to whom something is foretold, and not for the sake of the prophet. But it does not follow that another astrologer could therefore have such sure hits, or bring the art so high. For it should be well considered besides, that of such famous Practicas, if we weigh them []{.page .unprinted n=A3v}rightly, there are very few: most of them are not in themselves as they appear, but are often held to be true just as readily even if in some pieces they said the opposite. The reason is that we men call everything a hit that does not miss altogether; and as one is wont to forget the great number of daily misses, because they are nothing strange, so much the longer does one remember it when once someone hits after a fashion. Hence many a soothsayer's saying, however far it misses, is made true by other people's interpretation without his wishing it. As for natural prophecies, there are many and various kinds besides astrology, all of which busy themselves in rebus contingentibus, where what has gone before is commonly followed by the same thing, yet may also fail and turn out otherwise. And none of them is to be esteemed less than astrology itself: for although it takes its strong and unhindered causes of the various events from nature and the course of the heavens, it cannot do without the other natural foretellings, so that if it separated itself from all the others, and the astrologer would proclaim nothing but what his art can do, he would not only be understood by no one, but would also have little more to say to himself than he had learned in astronomy. They are also more certain than astrology, because they are taken from earthly and neighbouring causes, and can therefore come nearer ad speciem. In this way a man versed in war knows of future fortune or misfortune, a lawyer of the outcome of his cases, a statesman of changes of government, an old man of the future state of his children, a physician of the outcome of a sickness, a farmer of the hoped-for harvest or []{.page n=A4}of tomorrow's rain, each far better able to speak of his own matter than any astrologer: because the nearest related causes are well known to them, such as the troops of both commanders, provisions, munitions, mind and habit, the judge's practice, the good or bad laws of the land, the young people's manner and bearing and the look of their faces, the patient's natural strength, the winter weather, the nature of the soil, the evening and morning red, or the smell of the mountains: whereas the astrologer has only the one general and universal cause before him to look into, the future course of the heavens. Unless, then, a good astrologer were so experienced and practised in all temporal affairs that he could be compared with the best, and had besides the certain birth hours of all the foremost princes and lords: in which case I grant that such a man might foresee, though not quite specialia et individua, yet such things as would in every way be of great use to the diligent observer. But on the other hand it must be considered that in these few words just set down there lies such a breadth that hardly any man in his whole life reaches a fair part of it, and no one reaches the end, unless perhaps the Devil, now some five and a half thousand years old: and even this is not enough for him to arrive ad speciem, and so to prognosticate that he never fails, or can rely on it entirely: since besides the natural causes there is also the free will of men, of good and evil spirits, indeed of God himself, which act alongside nature and are effective in the events of every day, but can be foreseen by no creature, only by God. For although the will of man is for the most part bound per accidens to nature, and all spirits to God's decree, and God holds sway over nature, yet this binding is general, []{.page .unprinted n=A4v}and does not touch the single event on earth in specie (except God's providence); nor does it always go according to that order: rather God creates and ordains, and rules in men's hearts as he will, and also at times hinders the heavenly workings themselves by a miracle (though in my opinion not often), so that something comes about against all nature and can be foreseen by no creature, any more than what happens alongside it from natural causes. Therefore this still stands: that in the whole of nature no such sure, specified foretelling of future things as some demand of astrology is to be found, and that the Practicas are therefore not to be relied upon. But since, gracious and commanding Lords, in a well-ordered government not everything can or should be directed to gain and profit, but one must also uphold whatever otherwise serves God's honour and men's pleasure and adorns peace; and since this praiseworthy delight, which flows from astrology and from the contemplation of the divine order implanted in nature, has pleased these honourable Estates above other nations; and since Your Graces have always received such yearly Practicas with special favour: I have not wished to fall short this time in this part of my calling either, and have thought fit to dedicate and present this my fourth Prognostication to Your Graces in all humility and in the best hope. And I hereby humbly commend myself to you. Done at Graz, 1 September, in the year '97.

Your Graces'\
most humble and obedient\
M. Johannes Kepler.

## []{.page n=B}The First Chapter. Of the Weather, Crops and Sicknesses.

The astrologer should indeed err less in foretelling the weather than in other points, since it follows for the most part from nature and is least of all subject to the power and unsearchable will of men. That we nevertheless often miss the truth here too is largely the fault of astronomy, which is still deficient. In view of this I leave this point at the judgements set out at length in the calendar. Only this much is to be said: since the winter after Christmas appears for the most part warm, windy and wet, March foggy and cold, April natural, May, June and July hot, close and inclined to earthquakes, August foggy and melancholic, September wet, October and November mild, December again unnaturally warm, those skilled in farming and medicine can conclude better than I what a healthy and fruitful year this signifies for us. What the eclipses threaten concerning these points, see the third chapter. Experience of recent years has shown that bad constellations falling in the autumn (as next year at the end of August a conjunction of Saturn and Mars occurs) are harmful to the vintage and bring bad sicknesses among the people, just as the stars threaten the blossom in March when they bring it out.

## The Second Chapter. Of the Eclipses.

In the coming year '98 we are to expect three eclipses. The first, of the Moon, on 21 February in the 2nd degree of Virgo, at the Dragon's Tail, that is, in astronomical terms, at the crossing where the Moon runs over the path of the Sun towards the south. Its beginning is given by the calculation as a quarter before four in the morning. Its middle falls at half past five, when it is greatest; yet the Moon will not be wholly []{.page .unprinted n=Bv}darkened, but will keep a small red-coloured horn on its upper part, and shortly after, before it regains its natural light, will slip below the earth thus overturned. But further to the west, in Germany and beyond, its end will also be seen. Whoever likes to speculate further may take it as certain that at the beginning of the darkening he sees the shadow of America on the Moon, in the middle that of far Norway and Greenland, and at the end that of Spain and of Atlas in Africa. But I must not fail to mention here that the astronomical calculation is not entirely certain, and that this eclipse with its beginning, middle and end may well be half an hour or more later (as has often been noticed), so that the lesser part of it would be seen in our region. Its effect should, according to Ptolemy's opinion, extend over three and a half months; with us, however, it will begin around Christmas of '98 and end after two months.

Secondly there is a very great eclipse of the Sun on 7 March in the 16th degree of Pisces, at the Dragon's Head, namely at the crossing where the Moon runs over the path of the Sun northward and passes under the light for us. It should begin a quarter before nine and disappear again a little after eleven. The astronomical calculation makes it so great that no more than a 24th part of the Sun, that is a very small horn, should remain showing. If therefore it were possible for a man to stand somewhere in the heavens and look down on the Earth, he would see it darkened by a black round spot, which spot would sweep in a few hours in one stroke across the Canaries, Africa and Spain, Sardinia, Sicily, Greece, Egypt, Jerusalem, Babylon and Persia: so that in all the places named night must fall. Yet again it is to be understood that the astronomical calculation is such that, just as the lunar eclipse comes later, so this solar eclipse may well appear half an hour earlier and smaller, or perhaps quite covered. Be that as it may, this much is certain: that in our lands no greater darkening of the Sun has been seen in more than fifty years: so that all who are skilled in the art will have a fine spectacle, but the ignorant a terrifying one, and in broad daylight they will see the stars in the sky, especially Venus and Mercury after the Sun, and Jupiter at its []{.page n=B2}rising. But if it should happen that (as said) the Sun is wholly covered, or only that the sky is overcast with clouds, certain circumstances give us sure signs that no darker night will have been in the whole year than that day is to become dark. Yet such thick darkness does not last more than a quarter of an hour, though the whole darkening before and after stretches over two and a quarter hours. Its effect extends, according to Ptolemy's teaching, over two years and four months, namely from St John's Day in '98 to the autumn of 1600, and is strongest in July, August and September of 1599.

The third eclipse occurs on the Moon on 16 August, in the 24th degree of Aquarius, at the Dragon's Head: its beginning is below the earth at about half past six in the evening; at a quarter before eight, when the Moon has hardly come up, it is wholly extinguished, and stays darkened until an eighth of an hour before nine. From then on the light will gradually show itself again, and at last at an eighth of an hour after ten will appear in full. If all this should show itself later, that would be, as heard above, in keeping with past years and with experience. But since only those who live further to the east, and not we, will see the beginning of the eclipse, its effect, according to Ptolemy's rule, begins on that same day and extends into the following November. It should be mentioned that because both eclipses of the Moon take place at the rising and setting of the Sun and Moon, those skilled in the art will again have a wonderful spectacle. For since an eclipse of the Moon is nothing other than a shadow cast by the Earth, it necessarily follows that as soon as the one light rises above the Earth the other must at that very point go down, and in no way should both be seen at once during the darkening. But the night air, and especially the early morning air, has the nature of water or of a mirror: and it can happen on both occasions that through the watery vapour hanging over the mountains both lights appear at once, as if they were above the earth. Whoever likes may watch for this. So much for the three eclipses from the art of astronomy.

## []{.page .unprinted n=B2v}The Third Chapter. Of the Signification of the Eclipses and Other Aspects.

There are two things to judge, dear reader: the eclipses, and the other heavenly aspects or appearances. An eclipse, to tell the truth, has by its nature no effect at all, or only a slight and small one, in that it unexpectedly becomes night, and so cold. That it nevertheless signifies something seems to be because God the Lord lets the long-deserved punishments of men wait until nature gives an eclipse, upon which he then lets the punishments follow; either out of love for nature, his creation, and the order implanted in it, or so that the common people may have a visible warning in the heavens, and no one have any excuse. Be that as it may, all histories bear witness, as does Christ himself in Luke 21, that when the Sun and Moon are so darkened in the heavens, especially on earth in the following years, kings and princes are thrust from their rule by the revolt of their subjects, or hostile force, or death; good order is torn down, government disturbed, religion changed or assailed: and in all the histories one will hardly find an eclipse after which there did not soon follow the death or grievous trouble of one of the foremost heads of the world. Where that did not happen, such a great darkening of the Sun at least signified excessive heat, and with it earthquakes, in other places long rainy weather, with both hindering the crops and bringing dearth, and also pestilential sickness. But where, and with which person, such a thing will happen: that an astrologer cannot name from the mere sight of the eclipses. The astrologers do have many rules about the twelve houses, lordships and terms of the planets, but because they have no foundation they are not worth mentioning.

What further conjectures remain in nature are such that nothing can be concluded in specie; yet for the sake of greater diligence I will set them down too. First, since three great eclipses occur in one year, it is to be supposed that the evil foretold []{.page n=B3}will be all the greater. In the year 1544 there were four great eclipses, after which in the following years the Schmalkaldic War arose and Germany was sorely assailed. Secondly, if one holds, with many learned men, to the division of the lands among the twelve heavenly signs, one might refer the effect of the coming eclipse of the Sun, as the foremost, chiefly to the lands subject to the sign of Pisces: such as Portugal and the furthest part of Spain, Normandy in France, Calabria in Italy, in sum all lands that stretch out into the sea; but in Germany the two foremost cities from of old, lying on the Danube and the Rhine, namely Regensburg and Worms. Yet other places not named are not thereby exempt, since it is impossible to list all those subject to the sign. Whoever opens his eyes and sees where things are troubled and bad anyway will hit the mark better. In particular it should be mentioned here that in the year 1579 there was also an eclipse of eight points in the 16th degree of Pisces: after which, within a year, Henry, the last King of Portugal (where the eclipse also appeared much larger) died, and afterwards that same kingdom passed to the crown of Spain. Thirdly, since again a warm winter is to be expected, together with harmful constellations in March, June and autumn, the eclipses alone might perhaps threaten us with the continuation of a general sickness, and give occasion in some places to excessive rainy weather (because the eclipse falls in such a sign). That is enough about the eclipses.

Secondly, as for the rest of the course of the heavens, it has indeed its own strong effect, or rather its following on earth: since every changeable thing on earth orders itself in some measure by it, and changes with it through a natural sympathy. But because this effect is quite general, it makes the signification all the more obscure. For instance, the heavens are now and for the coming years arranged just as in the years 1538 and '39, in which the following events took place: 1. a truce between Spain and France; 2. new sects; 3. the death of several princely persons, and after it great change in Germany. And since in the coming year the foremost []{.page .unprinted n=B3v}constellations fall towards the cardinal points, and in particular in June all the planets stand with us in the north, in Taurus, Gemini, Cancer, Leo and Virgo, Jupiter and the Sun at their highest, the effect towards something new (if earthly affairs are fit for it) will be so much the stronger. Further, one might well look back to the year '83, at what new things have arisen since then in France, the Netherlands, at Cologne and Strasbourg, and elsewhere. For the coming eclipse appears at the place in the heavens where in that year the two highest planets came together, which now also bind their rays together upon us on earth in the manner of a trine. Thirdly, the signification of the eclipses will be so much the more violent because the bad constellation to be expected next 18 September and 24 December falls again close to the last two eclipses, and threatens grievously certain princes and confederated kings, so far as their nativities are rightly known. But whether in specie the Turkish war or another will continue, that does not lie with the stars, because, as said above, their effect is general: that is, because the heavens cannot make or keep war or peace, but rule what they find, so far as it lets itself be ruled by their nature and by God's decree. For as no hard stone, but only what is moist and watery, can be softened by the heavens and turned to vapour: so too, where there is lasting peace or other strong hindrances, the heavens can cause no unrest. In the nativities of particular persons, to be sure, such heavenly impulses to quarrel and anger occur: but to find out all the nativities is impossible for an astrologer, and they are themselves nothing but general significations about their persons. Therefore on this point the understanding will look far more to the nearest causes and consider how strong, able and united each side finds itself. Besides this, in the coming January swift events and alarms (perhaps through the death of a high person) may arise, carried on with deceitful counsels, but at last turning out happily to the advancement of our authorities; in February fair quiet; in March bad times, manly and daring deeds; in April disguised and wonderful []{.page n=B4}strokes; in May force and laborious undertakings, followed by swiftness, together with well-considered and secret counsel and the encouragement of our rulers; in June great movements in all men's desires and in everything that hangs on fortune; in July force pressing forward, with astonishment; after the beginning of August great misfortune, quarrels, defiance and alarm; in November good fortune for our authorities; in December danger to high persons, and an outbreak of every misfortune furthered by the heavens (per accidens): yet all only so far as like causes and dealings are present on earth too: for, as said, if by unavoidable necessity peace were made somewhere in the meantime, the heavens could do nothing more. And all this is to be understood in genere of all lands and matters in which fortune chiefly rules; only that the sign of Libra is given by the astrologers to the land of Austria in September, and for as long as Saturn runs in it. In sum, the heavens cannot do much harm to the stronger of two enemies, nor much good to the weaker. Whoever strengthens himself with good counsel, with troops, with arms, with courage, brings the heavens to his side too, and where they are against him, he overcomes them and all misfortune, etc.

May God Almighty grant us such a gracious new year, in which we are driven to moderation, sobriety, courage, prudence and all virtues, and are not given room to fill up the measure of our sins.

Luke, chapter 17.\
