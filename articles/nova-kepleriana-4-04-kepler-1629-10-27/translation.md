# Kepler to Müller, 17/27 October 1629

::: {.editorial-note}
English translation of the letter as printed in *Nova Kepleriana* 4 (1927), made for Plain Old Text. Each paragraph translates the one beside it.
:::

Many greetings.

Most famous and most friendly Sir.

Amid the ruins of towns, provinces, states and houses old and new, amid fears of barbarian invasions and of oppression at home, I am nevertheless compelled, as a nursling of Mars, indeed all but his chick, to hire printers, and either to pretend to begin the edition of Brahe's observations or even actually to begin it, stoutly hiding all fear. And I shall do it, with God's help, in the soldier's fashion: today exulting in orders, raging and storming, and leaving the care of my burial to tomorrow.

I am sending two young men, a compositor and a pressman, to buy a press at Leipzig, with M. Avianus showing them what is for sale there, and also to have type cast and paper made.

Our M. Bartsch, however, wrote these words on 10/20 October:

“To the Licentiate Müller, besides your money lately mentioned, the thirty-six joachimstalers, I have now given a hundred joachimstalers for safe keeping; so you can also claim mine from the Licentiate whenever you like, and I shall return his receipt.”

He does ask, if possible, that I first tell him what I have decided. But I need the money now, and cannot catch him as he flies off to Frankfurt. So I ask you, if you trust the bond we have formed, to pay out of my money and Bartsch's as much as will be needed under the instructions given to the said workmen; or, if you would rather keep your own affairs safe, then, once the 36 joachimstalers that are mine are spent, to give your guarantee for the rest for the time being, until Bartsch and I have had time to come to terms by letter.

Above all, as though a closer bond and friendship already existed between you, Sir, and me, I rest my whole hope on this: that you will direct the young men with your counsel and authority as they make contracts in my name, so that they contract carefully, and as sparingly as they can. I leave it to them to judge whether the press is fit. Whatever is done, I shall ratify without any complaint. As for the type, if it cannot be cast at Leipzig, it will have to be cast at Wittenberg, and the money sent there from Leipzig. Here I can only ask Your Excellency to give the one going there a letter of exchange to someone you know, whose readiness for this kind of service you are sure of. Or if such dealings are not usual among scholars, there will be no lack at Leipzig of a merchant of your acquaintance to deal, at your request, with a Wittenberg merchant, or, better, of a Leipzig printer to deal with the Wittenberg founder. You can give your word and guarantee without risk, since you hold Bartsch's money on deposit, or even trusting me. I also refer to you the account of the travelling money given to the young men: if you see that anything is lacking, for the one going to Sagan or the other going to Wittenberg, add something, and charge me with whatever they attest in their own hand; for they have had no more than four joachimstalers from me. And whatever it is unwise to trust to the one going to Wittenberg for the road, the man who pays out the other costs at Wittenberg can at least add there, at your request and on your guarantee.

I am not at all surprised at what Bartsch wrote about the Danish calculation. The principles on either side are such that I hardly know whether the eccentric longitude from the equinox about the sun can be the same at more than two places on the whole epicycle of Venus and Mercury. The Danish Astronomy, I think, does not have this eccentric epicycle, nor the unequal motion about the equant point, nor perhaps the varying distance of that point of attachment from the earth, as mine does; nor do I know whether the epoch of the epicycle's longitude is the same in both. I fixed it most circumspectly for our own times, most probably for Ptolemy's, and not without the witness of his observations; but I allowed myself the epoch of the Creation, assuming the uniform motion to be truly uniform, that is, without a secular equation, which in Saturn and Jupiter certainly exists to some degree, and probably in the sun: but of that another time.

One thing I add about the press. As long as my Patron's affairs are safe, however dangerous, I must certainly go on with the contract. If, however, as the fortune of war goes, some event should meanwhile occur by which the source of his generosity plainly appeared to be stopped (which, for the good of Christendom, I pray be far off), I should certainly think we must hold back. And so I entrust Your Excellency, as an intimate, in secret, with this instruction: in that case, do not let them go beyond some modest deposit, and keep the ratification reserved for me until they return; but before they leave, have everything arranged that will be needed for transport when I next ask, so that the press can be brought here safely even without an escort. Though winter is here, and winter quarters, and nothing of the kind is easily to be expected now; nor should uncertain rumours be lightly believed. Finally, know that I shall certainly ratify and welcome it even if you have concluded without such a precaution, which is all but impossible, or have completed the contract and paid out the money. For I would not give you grounds here for hesitating while things are uncertain, only for stopping, if things suddenly become quite certain.

Farewell, commended to God.

17/27 Oct. 1629. Sagan.

Your Excellency's\
most dutiful\
Johannes Kepler,\
Mathematician.

To the most famous and most excellent Mr\
Philipp Müller, Licentiate of Medicine,\
most celebrated Professor of Mathematics\
in the University of Leipzig, my friend,\
to be honoured with old loyalty,\
Leipzig.
