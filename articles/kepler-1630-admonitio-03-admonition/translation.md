## []{.page n=9}Admonition of Johannes Kepler, Imperial Mathematician

*To those curious about heavenly things: excerpted from the Ephemeris for the Year 1631.*

What I denied in my book, the Optical Part of Astronomy, ch. 8, p. 305, could happen in this century, that the star of Venus should cover a part of the Sun: the calculation shows that this will happen in this year 1631, on 6 December Gregorian. And although the matter is perhaps of little moment for prognostication, yet I, whose one aim is to enlarge astronomical knowledge by seeking out appearances of this kind, cannot but desire with all my prayers clear skies and the other circumstances suitable for observing it: not chiefly because such *incursions of Venus into the Sun* happen very rarely, not before a revolution of 235 years in the same place, and in no more than two places in the whole circuit, namely at each Node; so that from 23 May in the year 1526 it has never happened (here too the wording of the Optics must be corrected, which denied that it could have happened within 200 years), nor can it happen again until the year of the Incarnation 1761, on 25 May Julian: but for this reason, that if it should occur, and be rightly observed by []{.page n=10}those who will then be living, it will be able to teach everyone about the greatest matters in the art, which without this observation astronomers will scarcely ever bring to light.

Every day we gaze at the *planets'* little fires; all practitioners since Ptolemy and Hipparchus teach how to measure the apparent diameters of their little globes with suitable instruments; and here Tycho Brahe has yielded nothing to the ancients in skill and diligence.

But how far they were from certainty in the whole of this measurement has been taught us by that recent invention of the start of the century, the *Belgian Tube*, I mean, fitted with two lenses, concave and convex, and taught astronomy by *Galileo* of Florence: which, by wiping away the excess of light, shows the *diameters of the planets* many times smaller, when comparison is made with other visible things, all of which the tube itself does enlarge. After the diameters of the stars began to be determined more exactly by this tube, I began to turn my mind to tracing the proportion of the planetary globes among themselves. Yet I could not accomplish what I sought by way of demonstration, unless I adopted some principles that were probable but undemonstrated, for which see the *Epitome of Copernican Astronomy*, bk 4, pp. 484, 485.

But from this method, whose foundation is that the size of the planetary bodies is in proportion to their distances from the Sun, arose Precept 110 on p. 75 of the Rudolphine Tables, as the Reader is told at the end of the precept. And yet we do not have matters quite clear. For the diameter of the body of Mars ought to have grown to as much as 6 minutes when it is nearest the Earth in Aquarius: but the tube, applied in the year 1623 in Capricorn, was seen to reject so great a diameter, on comparing the diameters of ♂ and ♀. And so in my Hyperaspistes of Tycho I myself indicated that I must consult further with Galileo; and *Johannes Remus Quietanus* himself, to whose view I had gone over in the said Epitome, p. 485, now departs from it; and, retaining meanwhile some small part of the probability adopted, []{.page n=11}which, if it does not make the bodies proportional to the distances, at least makes the diameters of all six equal among themselves, for the rest he moves Mars, even in its acronychal position, so far from the Earth that it produces an appearance no greater than that to which the tube testifies. And by these principles he brings it about that, whereas I long ago tripled the old distance of the Sun and the Earth, this one of mine must now in turn be quadrupled, and so the old Ptolemaic one multiplied twelvefold.

But since the size of the world's edifice, through this change of principles and its connection with the testimony of the tube, is called wholly into doubt, I think we must look back to our assumptions; and see also whether perhaps one thing about these matters may be true, beautiful and good, and another has seemed probable to us. For what if that equality, which we demanded as the only beautiful thing, whether in the proportion of the globes' spherical space to the distances, or in the diameters themselves; if this (I say) is either not to be held the only beautiful thing, or lies rather in the matter of the globes, and in their weight as it were, while a greater density of the globe contracts the diameter of Mars, so that this diameter of the Martian globe is truly smaller than that of the terrestrial globe, and in that way produces an appearance consonant with the testimony of the tube? For in this way a globe of ice from water, equal in weight to a globe of gold, fills almost eighteen times the space which the gold occupies; the diameters standing in the proportion which is between the numbers 21 and 8, roughly.

But if, on the other hand, from other arguments drawn from the periodic time of each planet (Epitome of Astronomy, bk 4, pp. 486, 487) so much strength has come to these fine-looking foundations that they can hardly be uprooted in the minds of those who philosophize: then on the other side I have long been meaning to ask Galileo, if I were given the chance of speaking with him, whether full provision has been made in the handling of the tube, so that no optical spectre can deceive us here? Of Mars I remember that Galileo used to complain in the early days that its disc was shown []{.page n=12}indeterminate and blunted, as it were, and not polished to a clean edge. Whether he has made progress from this in observing, I do not know. To me, certainly, it seemed to appear round enough. But with Venus, when she is below the Sun, the reliability of observations labours more: for her face, around the limits of the greatest elongations, thins to a semicircle, and lower down to a horn. To say nothing now of the estimate of the space, of the hemisphere which the tube takes in at one view; nothing of its comparison with the diameter of the planet; nothing of the application and bringing back of the eye, of oblique viewing, of unsteadiness.

To whichever side, then, our suspicions may lean, this *Observation* would do very much indeed to strengthen or dispel them: if we should succeed in drawing on paper, by applying the tube, the globe of *Venus* immersed in the very disc of the Sun, and in putting the proportion of its diameter to the Sun's diameter before our very eyes to be judged. For since Venus waxes and wanes in light like the Moon, it is certain that she is an opaque body, and so blocks the rays of the Sun on the side where she passes beneath, and presents the appearance of a round spot: whose diameter, as in the example of Precept 110 cited above, should contain 7 minutes 6 seconds, a little less than a quarter of the diameter of the Sun: if indeed those assumptions of ours are as true as they have seemed probable to us.

And although the calculation gives the *mid-moment of the conjunction* as 9 hours 41 minutes, when the Sun with us has already spent 6 hours below the horizon, and for that reason assigns this appearance to America: yet who, for this position of Venus, will warrant the reliability of the calculation to the fineness of half a degree, which can be made up by an error of 6 or 7 minutes in the eccentric longitude from the equinox?

This is cause enough for me *to urge each and all*, not only *ship-masters* who will sail the Ocean, or *learned men* living in America, in Mexico and the neighbouring provinces; but Europeans too, *Professors of Mathematics* established in the universities, and also *Magnates* who have leisure for these []{.page n=13}delights of heavenly spectacles; in short all to whom heavenly things are a concern: that from this very time each should get himself a *Tube* able to reveal the *spots of the Sun*, accustom himself to handling it, and know the length to which its lenses must be drawn apart that brings the spots into view most sharply. For that length is a little greater than the one that serves the sight for looking through: and the sign of the most correct arrangement that there can be in any instrument is if the edges of the spots begin to be tinged on either side with the colours of the rainbow: whatever is beyond or short of this falls away from perfection.

Nor, I know, will your simple tube without lenses, Most Illustrious Prince and Lord, Lord Philip, Landgrave of Hesse, stand idle here; whose construction is such that it can show, most readily on its white tablet, the diameter of this round spot, to be taken off even with compasses: and if to this is finally added the diameter of the hole through which the Sun's ray has entered, it will be most correct, and free of that distortion which the lower concave lens produces in a viewing tube.

Churches too will serve the clergy, if that order has any students of the art. For in the year 1613, at Regensburg, in the cathedral church, I saw, and pointed out to acquaintances standing by, *traces of the Sun's spots* in all the round rays falling from on high through chinks in the windows. To say nothing of that ruinous house, hollow inside and spacious, in which at Prague in the year 1607, having caught a ray of the Sun tinged with a notable spot, I proclaimed, in a booklet and verses published to the world, but falsely, that *Mercury* had been seen *in the Sun*: as I afterwards confessed in the Prolegomena to these Ephemerides. For the conjunction of the Sun and Mercury was many days off, on the testimony of calculation. So that was a spot on the Sun: certainly because, as the observation has it, it was neither exactly round nor evenly shaded on all sides.

With whichever of these aids each may be equipped, let him set before himself this one thing to do, wherever []{.page n=14}on earth he may be: that for two days running, which will be 6 and 7 December Gregorian style, he look again and again at these rays of the Sun, falling through the lenses of the Tube or through a simple hole; and whatever he observes, let him share it with other mathematicians, should clouds perhaps have hindered any of them, and so with me myself, if I am alive.

Nor is this entry of Venus beneath the Sun the only one; indeed, you may be surprised to hear, thirty days earlier, no more, the calculation also shows *Mercury in the Sun*, namely on 7 November Gregorian, the day before a very great eclipse of the Moon, and at a little more than an hour after noon, and so visible in Europe. So the care of observers will also take in this three-day span of 6, 7, 8 November. For though this entry of Mercury beneath the Sun has more frequent occasions, yet something greater than in the case of Venus must be held in doubt about the reliability of the calculation of longitude: since suitable observations fail us, the planet lying mostly hidden under the Sun. So let the diligence of observing every conjunction that can be observed make up for the defect of the calculation about all of them.

*Parallax* by day, if there is to be any, will be four times the Sun's in Venus, in Mercury about one and a half times. And in both cases it helps and prolongs its phenomenon. For since each planet grazes the northern edge of the Sun, the parallax, moving them to the south, will bring them nearer the centre of the Sun.

*These from Kepler's Ephemerides.*
