## []{.page .unprinted n=61}Solid Geometry of the Austrian Barrel, Part II. The solid geometry of the Austrian barrel in particular.

### To which kind of the foregoing figures the shape of the Austrian barrel belongs.

Having set out, then, the general matters on the solid geometry of regular figures, both from Archimedes and from my own findings, that seemed useful for understanding the proofs, I now come closer to my purpose; and many things likewise untouched by Archimedes, on parallelepipeds in the same sphere and their cylinders and cones, but which seemed to concern the nature of the Austrian barrel alone, I bring in under the title of the Solid Geometry of the Austrian Barrel, and join them to the foregoing supplement to Archimedes. For the shape of a barrel is a cylinder with a belly; or, to speak more exactly, a barrel is understood as divided into two frustums, as it were, of two cones, whose vertices, pointing in opposite directions, are understood to be cut off by the wooden heads of the barrel, while their common base, dividing the cones, is the widest circle through the barrel's belly.

In Schema XVIII, given here, the cylinder is understood as HEGF; the cone as ABC, and another equal to it from AC towards ND; the vertices cut off as EBG, and another equal to it from HF towards ND; the frustums as AEGC, AHFC, with the common base AC.

What is true of cylinders and of frustums of cones, then, can also be applied to the shape of a barrel, since it departs little from a cylinder and less from a conical frustum, while the leaning together of the staves, here understood by the straight line CRF, bends outwards into some bulge.

Most exactly, the shape of every barrel is the middle frustum either of a lemon from a segment of a circle, or of a plum from a vertical part of an ellipse, or of a parabolic spindle, but mostly of a hyperbolic one, with equal vertices cut off at both ends. The reason why I say a hyperbolic spindle is this: because barrels mostly take their bulge in the middle of the belly, while towards the ends and the wooden heads on both sides they come nearer to conical straightness, so that the hoops can more easily be pushed and tightened by driving. And the hyperbola does this, and the conoid and spindle born from it, so that its arms gradually degenerate from the middle bend into the straightness of the asymptotes. The parabolic spindle and the elliptical plum do the same in part, but the hyperbolic spindle most plainly; the elliptical plum less and less, and not every one, but only a slender one, from a vertical segment of an ellipse whose axis, after cutting, does not reach the focus; and this caution also has place in the parabolic spindle. But the olive, created from the middle segment of an ellipse between its vertices, does the opposite: for it bends more towards the ends than in the middle, which is foreign to the shape of a barrel. Though I would not deny that, because of the imperceptible difference between these figures, a barrel sometimes has the shape of a frustum of an olive too; but made not by the cooper's design but by a slip of the hand. But I think no barrel has ever been made []{.page .unprinted n=62}from the belly of Archimedes' spheroid, which Clavius put forward as nearest the truth (the others, whose generation I taught above, being not yet known), though ready meanwhile (Clavius's words) to accept gladly and gratefully a more exact one if anyone should find it. For the long spheroid, which has in the middle a proper bulge suited to barrels, bends too much towards the cut-off vertices, and no hoops could hold on it for long. But if you take the middle belly of a very slender spheroid, you will indeed lessen this trouble of too much bending at the ends of the barrel, but in turn you allow the barrel no belly, as if you built it from a pure and simple cylinder.

### How one might convict the gauging rod of falsity, and how its reliability is established.

To return, then, to the beginning of this inquiry: my first objection to the gauging rod was this, that the same length AF of it would fit barrels of unlike shapes, whose spaces would nevertheless not be equal.

To show this in a plane, it seemed best to take, in place of the body of the cylinder, the parallelogram by which the cylinder is cut []{.page .unprinted n=63}through its axis. For what is true of the cylinder can also be applied to the conical frustum AHFC and its trapezium that cuts it through the axis, namely to the plane AHFC, in which the gauging rod AF also lies. For this plane seemed to grow and shrink with the body of the cylinder. Let there be, then, on this matter

### Theorem I.

Sections through the axis of right cylinders that have equal diagonals have unequal areas, unless the ratio of the diameter of the base to the height is the same or reversed; and among them the greatest area is that of the section of the cylinder whose height equals the diameter of its base.

Let the diameter of the cylinder's base be CI, and the height of the cylinder IA, equal to the diameter of the base, now representing half a wine barrel; let the section of the cylinder be the rectangle AICO, which here is a square, with the diagonal line AC representing the gauging rod going down slantwise from the filling-hole A to the foot C of the wooden head IC. And since the cylinder is supposed right, CIA will be a right angle. Let AC be bisected at N, and about the centre N with radius NA let the semicircle AIC be described, which will pass through I, because AIC is a right angle. And since AI, IC are equal, the arcs AI, IC will be quadrants of the circle; and when the points N, I are joined, the angles INA, INC will be right angles, and IN perpendicular to AC.

![](/img/stereometria/p63-1.webp){alt="Schema XIX: the square AICO with its diagonal AC, and the semicircle AIC on AC through the points Q, I, H, D, B and P, with lines from them to A and C"}

Now let any points of one quadrant be chosen, say H and B, and let them be joined to the ends A, C of the diameter by the lines HA, HC, BA, BC: so that, the same diagonal AC remaining, the square AICO, or its half, the triangle AIC, is changed into other figures AHC, ABC, the angle staying right at H and B as at I, since all lie in the same semicircle; so that AHC, ABC are again the halves of sections of right cylinders, and the diameters of their bases are now CH, CB and the heights of the cylinders HA, BA.

I say that the area AIC is the greatest, AHC less, and ABC (the point B being further from I, the end of the quadrant) less again.

For let perpendiculars HM, BK be let fall from the points H, B onto the diameter AC. Then, by what is proved in Euclid, the area of each triangle equals the rectangle under half the base AC and the height of the triangle, namely NI, MH, KB. So as IN is to HM and BK, so is the area AIC to the areas AHC []{.page .unprinted n=64}and ABC. But in the quadrant AI all straight lines parallel to the radius IN, such as HM, BK, are less than the radius IN itself, and BK, further from it, less than HM, nearer to it. The area AHC, then, is less than AIC, and ABC less again than AHC. And so the rectangles that are twice these triangles are greater in the same order.

I say also that cylinders that have the ratio of height to base diameter reversed have equal sections.

For let AB be the diameter of the base and BC the height of the cylinder: it is plain that the triangle ABC, which is half the section of the cylinder through the axis, stays the same as before, when BC was the diameter of the base and AB the height, and so the ratio of these lines reversed.

Further, I must not hide the error into which a careless consideration of this theorem threw me on the first day. For mentioning it will warn the reader to beware of similar things elsewhere too. For I reasoned wrongly thus: since the ratio of similar areas is the square of the ratio of the sides, and that of similar bodies the cube, so also in dissimilar figures using the same diagonal AC the ratio of the bodies would always be analogous to the ratio of the areas and lines. But this is false; and if I had advised the coopers always to make the diameter of the wooden head half the length of the staves, as the safety of measuring the area requires, as if the safety of measuring the body were so provided for too, I should have done their craft great harm and led them further from the mark. For where the area of the plane cutting the cylinder is greatest, there the body of the cylinder is not also greatest. But that will appear later. Now I shall apply what has been said of the right cylinder to the conical frustum too.

### Theorem II.

In conical frustums everything else remains, except that among the frustums next to the one whose side equals the diameter of its base the ratio of their areas varies more than if they were cylinders instead of conical frustums, and among those further away less.

For since the angle contained by the side of the frustum and the diameter of the lesser base is greater than a right angle, it will fit not in a semicircle but in an arc less than a semicircle. Let PQ be drawn parallel to AC, then, cutting the semicircle at the points P, Q, and the perpendiculars IN, HM, BK at the points R, S, V; and let the points I, H, B be joined with the points P, Q. PQ now represents the gauging rod; QI, QH, QB the diameter of the base of the cut cone; IP, HP, BP the side of the frustum, half the length of the staves in barrels; and the angles QIP, QHP, QBP, obtuse and equal to each other, since they stand in the same segment PQI, give an equal cause through all these figures for the variation I have undertaken to explain here.

Again, then, the area PIQ is to the areas PHQ and PBQ as IR to HS and BV; and since equal amounts RN and SM have been taken from the unequal IN, HM, []{.page .unprinted n=65}the remainders IR and HS will be in a greater ratio: the difference of the areas PIQ, PHQ is therefore more noticeable than that of the areas AIC, AGC. On the other hand, the decreases of the perpendiculars are greatest at A, so they will be less at P. And at P the perpendiculars of the frustums vanish, but at A those of the cylinders: the areas PBQ near the end P therefore decrease in a smaller ratio than the areas ABC near the end A. But earlier, near the beginning I, PHQ decreased in a greater ratio than AHC. This theorem is especially notable because of another, longer-lasting delusion about comparing conical frustums with one another, which will be mentioned below.

Further, the refutation of the said delusion is contained in the following

### Theorem III.

The bodies of right cylinders whose sections have the same diagonal do not have to each other ratios analogous to the ratios of the areas by which they are cut through the axis; nor is the body greatest of the one whose cutting area is greatest.

In the previous schema, since AIC is half the area IO cutting the cylinder IO through the axis, and IC is the diameter of the base: multiplying the area AICO by IC, the diameter of the base, creates the rectangular parallelepiped that encloses the cylinder. As 14 to 11, then, so is this parallelepiped to the body of its cylinder, by III of the foregoing solid geometry of regular figures. So in whichever figure, of those with the same diagonal AC, this parallelepiped is greatest, there the cylinder too is greatest. But in the figure of which AIC is half the section this parallelepiped is not greatest, however much the cutting area AICO is greatest; which I prove thus.

Let there be in the quadrant IA a point H next to the point I, that is, at the end of the quadrant. Since AHC is a different figure from AIC and has the same diagonal AC, and the areas AIC, AHC are to each other as their perpendiculars IN, HM, they will be in the least ratio to each other at the end of the quadrant, and very nearly equal, because the lines IN, HM, a fixed distance apart, are also in the least ratio to each other; and this ratio between them always becomes greater the nearer they come to A, the beginning of the quadrant, the same distance between them remaining.

But to create the body, the lines CI, CH must be multiplied by the areas AIC, AHC; and equivalently, as the rectangle under NI, IC is to the rectangle under MH, HC, so is the body of the parallelepiped AICI to the body AHCH. But the ratio of HC to CI is greater than that of IN to HM. For CI subtends the quadrant CH, a little more, and their halves are the perpendiculars of half the quadrant, and a little more; and these are not in the least ratio to each other, since that is greater in the middle of the quadrant than at the end, the same distance of the perpendiculars being supposed on both sides. So, the distance of the perpendiculars being the same, namely half the arc HI, the perpendiculars about the end of the quadrant I are in a smaller ratio than the halves of CI, CH about the middle of the quadrant, also half the arc HI apart. And since the difference of the halves of CI, CH is greater than that of the perpendiculars at I, which are half the arc HI apart, []{.page .unprinted n=66}the difference of the whole CI, CH, twice the former, will be greater than that of the perpendiculars IN, HM, the whole arc HI apart. So it follows that the difference between IC and CH is greater than between HM and IN. So although HM of the second figure is a little less than IN of the first, CH of the second figure in turn is much greater than CI of the first. The rectangle MHC, then, is greater than NIC, and so the cylinder and its parallelepiped AHCH greater than AICI; whereas, on the contrary, the cutting area AHC of the cylinder AHCH was before less than AIC. The ratio of the bodies AICI, AHCH, therefore, is not analogous to the ratio of the areas AIC, AHC. And AIC is indeed the greatest of the areas on the same diagonal, but the body AICI is not the greatest; AHCH is greater than it.

### The reason for the proportion used in making the Austrian barrel.

### Theorem IV.

Of all the parallelepipeds or []{.page .unprinted n=67}columns inscribed in the same sphere which have two opposite square bases, the cube has the greatest body.

The theorem has been wanting until now, though it has an evident showing from analogy. The circle is the most capacious of all plane figures contained by equal perimeters, as Pappus proved in Book V. And of plane figures contained by an equal number of sides and an equal perimeter, those more like the circle are more capacious.

Again, of segments from different circles whose circumferences are equal, the semicircle is the most capacious.

In the same way the cube is the most capacious of all solids contained by surfaces equal to its own. And of polyhedra of equal surface, the more like a sphere each is in the order and number of its faces, the more capacious it is: the icosahedron indeed the most capacious, because it is contained by the most bases, as the circle by infinitely many bases, as it were. Pappus has all this in Book V.

And of segments of different globes with equal surfaces Archimedes proved that the hemisphere is the most capacious of all segments of a globe contained by an equal surface.

So the nature of the circle and the globe excels the other figures when they are of equal perimeter. But when equality of surface is set aside for them, and in its place the same sphere is circumscribed about the polyhedra, the opposite happens to some, so that the dodecahedron is greater than the icosahedron, as Apollonius and Hypsicles proved in their additions to Euclid; but this happens because of the same nature of the globe, and because of the likeness of the figure to the globe that reigns here. For before, when the surfaces were equal, the likeness to the globe lay in the number of faces; here, since an equal sphere is set for the corners in the various figures, and their arrangement ordered by it, the likeness to the globe lies in the number of corners, which is greater in the dodecahedron than in the icosahedron.

This being so, it is easy to see that among bodies contained by an equal number of faces too, the one more like the globe would be more capacious in the globe, where the likeness lies in the equality and similarity of the faces and the order of the corners. These belong to the cube above the other parallelepipeds of the globe. So the cube will be more capacious. This is the showing from analogy. I now add a full proof, which indeed has some difficulty from the fact that small sections of a solid have to be imagined in a plane schema.

In the schema just below let AGB be the great circle of the sphere, its diameter, and also the axis of the sphere, AB; and let AG be the side of the cube in the sphere and GB the diagonal of one square face of the cube. Columns with two square bases inscribed in the same sphere, then, are either taller than the cube, with square bases smaller than the cube's, or lower than the cube, with wider square bases.

[]{.page .unprinted n=68}First let the column be taller than the cube. Let a longer line DF be drawn in the circle parallel to GA, the height of the cube, cutting GB at K; and likewise from the point D, the end of the height of the column, let DE be drawn parallel to GB, the diagonal of the cube's square, DE being the diagonal of the column's square. I say that the body of the column FDE is less than the body of the cube AGB. For DK is to BK as GK to KF; and alternately, as FK to KB, so GK to KD. But AG is less than FK, and GB greater than KB. So the ratio of AG to GB is greater than that of FK to KB; and so the ratio of the same AG to GB is greater than that of GK to KD. But AG to GB is the ratio 1 : √2. So the ratio of GK to KD is less than 1 : √2, and GK is either more in square than half of KD, or equals KD, or is even longer than it.

![](/img/stereometria/p68-1.webp){alt="Schema XX: a circle with diameter BA through the centre C, three upright chords, and pairs of parallel lines from points D, G and L near the top down to E, B and M on the left and N, A and F on the right"}

Now an addition has been made to the height of the cube's body by multiplying two of the column's squares, one at each end, by the little piece of height KD. Conversely a reduction has been made from the thickness of the cube's body by multiplying four squares, equal to the column's, round the body of the column, by the little decrease by which half the side of the cube's square differs from half the side of the column's square; and this decrease is to GK, the decrease of the half diagonal, as AG to GB, namely 1 : √2 of it. Yet not all the reduction is expressed here, because round these four slabs of square kind, smaller than the cube's square, there lie twelve more little columns, which likewise have been taken from the thickness of the cube. So if the four slabs round the column, taken from the body of the cube, are greater than the two slabs added to the body of the cube above and below, much more will the whole reduction from the body of the cube exceed the addition made to the height. And the four side slabs are greater than the two height slabs, which I prove thus. Any one of the side slabs is to a height slab as the decrease of the cube's side to the increase of the height, namely the increase whose half is KD. But the decrease of the cube's side has to the increase of the height a ratio compounded of the ratio of AG to GB and of GK to KD. For as the decrease of the half side is to the decrease GK of the half diagonal, so is AG to GB, which is one part of the ratio; the other is the ratio GK to KD itself. And these two ratios joined make something less than 1 : 2. For AG to GB is 1 : √2, and GK to KD less than 1 : √2. And a half and less than a half, put together, make less than a whole. If, then, one side slab has to one height slab a ratio less than 1 : 2, one height slab is not []{.page .unprinted n=69}fully twice one side slab, and one height slab does not fully equal two side slabs, but is less; and consequently two height slabs are less than four side slabs. And so the addition made to the height is less than the reduction made from the sides of the cube. A column in the sphere taller than the cube, therefore, is less than the body of the cube.

Second, let the column be lower than the cube, and let LN, shorter, be drawn in the circle parallel to GA, the height of the cube, for the height of the column; and from L let LM be drawn parallel to GB, the diagonal of the cube's face, for the diagonal of the column's square, greater than the cube's, cutting GA at O.

Again, then, as before, as MO is to OA, so is GO, the decrease of the height on one side, to OL, the increase of the half diagonal. But BG is less than MO, and GA greater than OA. So the ratio of BG to GA, which in the cube is √2 : 1, is less than GO to OL. The ratio of GO to OL is therefore greater than √2 : 1.

But the ratio between OL, the increase of the half diagonal, and the increase of the half side of the square is √2 : 1. So, √2 and more than √2 being compounded into one, the ratio of GO, the decrease of half the height, to the increase of the half side will be greater than 2 : 1. But by multiplying two of the cube's squares by GO, the two slabs taken from the height of the cube's body are created. Conversely, by multiplying four of the cube's squares by the increase of the half side, four slabs are created which are greater than the addition and surrounding made round the body of the cube. For although when these four slabs have been added the column still gapes, four little columns being wanting at the four upright edges of the column, yet in turn these slabs exceed the height of the column by eight other little columns of the same height as the wanting ones, but thicker than they. For the thickness of these is GO, while the thickness of those is to OL as AG to GB, that is, less than OL, and so much less than GO. On three counts, then, the eight thick exceeding little columns are greater than the four thin wanting ones. The four said slabs, therefore, are greater than the addition made to the body of the cube. So if the increase of the half side were exactly half of GO, what is created by GO multiplied by two squares would be equal to what is created by the increase of the half side multiplied by four squares. But the increase of the half side is less than half of GO, as was proved. So the four square slabs are less than the two height slabs. Much less, then, is the addition made to the sides of the cube than the reduction made from the height. A column in the sphere lower than the cube of the sphere, therefore, is less than the body of the cube in the same sphere. And before, the column taller than the cube was also less than it: so no column in the sphere, with square bases and rectangular sides, reaches the body of the cube in the same sphere: which was to be proved.

### Theorem V.

Of all cylinders having the same diagonal, the greatest and most capacious is the one whose base diameter is to its height in the ratio √2 : 1, or []{.page .unprinted n=70}as the side of the tetrahedron, or the diagonal of the cube's face, to the side of the cube in the same sphere.

Let the schema of the first theorem be repeated, and let AC be the diagonal line of the rectangle by which the cone is cut through the axis, representing the gauging rod; and on AC let the semicircle AGC be set up, and let AC be divided into three equal parts, AL being one part and LO two; and from L let the perpendicular LG be raised, cutting the circle at G, and let G be joined to A and C. Since, then, GL is twice LA, CA will be three times LA. And as CA is to AG, so is AG to LA. And when three quantities are in continued proportion, as the first is to the third, so is the square of the first to the square of the second; so since CA, the first, is three times LA, the third, the square of the diameter CA will also be three times the square of AG, the second. And since the square of AC equals the squares of AG and GC together, of which AG is a third part, GC will be two thirds of AC, and so the square of GC will be twice the square of AG. AG, then, is the side of the cube inscribed in the sphere AGC, and AC is the diagonal of the cube's square, or the side of the tetrahedron inscribed in the same sphere. I say that the cylinder whose base diameter is GC and height GA is the most capacious, or of greatest body, of all cylinders that have this diagonal AC. For since G and C are points on the surface of the sphere, and the line GC is the diameter of one base, the whole circumference of that base will stand on the surface of the sphere; and so too the opposite base, of which A is one point. But if AG is the side of the cube and GC the diagonal of a face of the cube, a face of the cube will necessarily be inscribed in the circle GC, and so in the sphere, with its two opposite corners G and C; and so too in the circle of the opposite base drawn through A. So the cylinder now defined will have inscribed in it a cube of the same height as itself, all of whose corners stand on the surface of the sphere.

![](/img/stereometria/p70-1.webp){alt="Schema XXI: the semicircle AGC of Schema XIX drawn as a full circle on the diameter AC, with the perpendicular LG, the lines GA and GC, dotted circles, and squares drawn below on the line from C"}

In the same way, in any other circle of the sphere, whose diameter is, say, IC, the square of a column is understood as inscribed, whose []{.page .unprinted n=71}height is IA, and two opposite corners of one square are I and C, and of the other square A and X: so a column of the same height is inscribed in the cylinder AIC.

But the ratio of all columns to the cylinders of equal height in which they are inscribed is the same; and the cube is the greatest of all columns in the sphere; so the cylinder AGC, circumscribed about the cube of the sphere, is the greatest of all the other cylinders in the sphere, such as AIC.

The same proof can also be set up by Schema XIX[^p71a] in this way. Let three cylinders end at the points H, G, A, having the same diagonal CA, bases CH, CG, CB and heights HA, GA, BA. Let the square of CG be twice the square of GA, and CH shorter and CB longer than CG. Let perpendiculars HM, GL, BK come down onto CA. Since, then, CGA is a right angle, as the square of CG is to the square of GA, so is the straight line CL to the straight line LA, namely twice it; and as the square of CH to the square of HA, so CM to MA; and as the square of CB to the square of BA, so CK to KA. And as the squares CH, CG, CB are to each other, so are the circular bases of the cylinders to each other. So as CM, CL, CK are to each other, so too are the bases of the cylinders to each other. And the ratio of cylinders is compounded of the ratio of the bases and the ratio of the heights. So the three rectangles under CM, CL, CK, which have the ratio of the bases, and under the heights HA, GA, BA, have among themselves the ratio of the cylinders.

Alternately, as AM, AL, AK are to each other, so too are the squares of the heights AH, AG, AB. The same quantity LM, then, is added to LA and taken from LC; and in turn the same quantity LK is taken from LA and added to LC. And since CL is twice LB, the ratio of the shorter CM to the longer CL is greater than half the reversed ratio of MA, longer by as much, to the shorter LA. As if CL is 20 and LA 10, then CM 19 and MA 11, the ratio 20 to 19 is greater than half the ratio 11 to 10, that is, 22 to 20. For the ratio 22 to 20 has two elements, 22 to 21 and 21 to 20, each of which is less than the ratio 20 to 19. The ratio of MA to LA, then, and so of the square of HA to the square of GA, is less than the square of the ratio CL to CM. But the ratio of the straight lines HA to GA themselves is half that of the squares, and half of a squared ratio is the simple ratio. So the ratio of the height HA to the height BA is less than LC to MC, the ratio of the bases. The rectangle under HA, CM, then, representing the cylinder CHA, is less than the rectangle under GA, CL, representing the cylinder CGA, because CM is shorter in its ratio than HA is longer in its.

The same is proved by reversed arguments of the cylinder CBA too. For the ratio of the longer CK to the shorter CL is less than half the reversed ratio of the shorter AK to the longer AL. As if CL is 20 and LA 10, then CK 21 and KA 9. The ratio 20 to 21 is less than half that of 9 to 10, or 18 to 20. For the two elements of the ratio 18 to 20, 18 to 19 and 19 to 20, are each greater than the ratio 20 to 21. The ratio of LA to AK, then, that is, of the square of GA to the square of AB, is greater than the square of KC to CL; and so the ratio of the lines GA to AB is greater than KC to CL; and CK is not as much longer than CL in []{.page .unprinted n=72}its ratio as BA is shorter than GA in its; and the rectangle under BA, CK is less than the rectangle under GA, CL; the cylinder CBA, then, is less than the cylinder CGA: so CGA alone is the greatest of all.

### Corollary I.

Cylindrical barrels without a belly, whether of a longer shape than the Austrian or a shorter, are less capacious than Austrian ones.

### Corollary II.

It is plain from this that it was by some good and geometrical genius that the Austrian coopers keep this rule for building a barrel: that they use a third of the length of a stave for the radius of the wooden head. For in this way it comes about that the cylinder sketched in the mind between the two wooden heads has its two halves as near as possible to the rule of Theorem V, and sharing in the most capacious figure, even if they depart somewhat from the perfection of the rule. For other figures, ending at points next to G on this side and that, vary the capacity very little, because the capacity of the figure AGC is the greatest; and those standing about the greatest on either side have at first imperceptible decreases.

In the following schema let CG have to GA the ratio √2 : 1, namely that of 100000 to 70711: AG doubled ought by this rule to have 141421; but the coopers take for this a length of 150000, one and a half times the base, for the length of the stave GX, which is a little more than 141421. And this very thing makes for a more exact imitation of the most capacious figure. For the staves both curve and stand out at both ends with their margins, running out beyond the grooves with which they take hold of and grip the wooden heads. What is too much in this length of the staves, which is to the diameter of the base as 3 to 2, is set down to these margins, which were not counted in examining the figure by the rule of Theorem V.

Who would deny that nature teaches geometry by instinct alone, even without reasoning, when our coopers, led only by their eyes and a sense of beauty, have learned to give half a barrel the most capacious shape? Let a geometer come forward and teach an easier method of building a barrel whose half comes closer to the most capacious cylinder than this one, in the ratio 3 : 2, which the Austrian coopers have held from of old; let the same geometer teach a shape better suited to quick measurement than the one Austria builds.

I could have believed that there was once some most excellent geometer in Austria who taught the coopers these things, had I not been held back by this: that no trace of so beautiful a proof is to be found anywhere in the books of the geometers; and that this way of building a barrel, as far as I know, is not used on the Rhine and in the other wine-growing places, for they mostly make longer barrels. How could it be likely that what is not publicly received was taken by one nation alone from the books or teaching of geometers?

### []{.page .unprinted n=73}A warning.

Who is so sharp and circumspect in mind that, having struggled free of that delusion by which he had given the greatest body, among cylinders with the same diagonal, to those whose section through the axis has the greatest area; and having learned that the greatest body belongs to those cylinders in which the base diameter is √2 times the height, by this Theorem V, but the greatest area of section to those in which these lines are equal, by Theorem I of this part; and indeed that the case is the same as regards the areas of conical frustums too, by Theorem II of this part: who, I say, having seen these things, would not at once presume of the body of a conical frustum the same as of the body of a cylinder, namely that the body of that conical frustum too is greatest in which the diameter of the lesser base is √2 times the sloping side? And I both believed this for the last year and a half and followed it; and I was on the point, relying on this foundation, of ranking all Rhenish barrels without distinction, regardless of their bellies, below the Austrian in judging their capacity; which I should have done with no injury to them, but yet not with equal right. So I credit it to the convenience of having the press here that geometry plucked my ear here as I saw to the printing, and supplied me with the following theorems, as an enlarging supplement to Archimedes; by which at the same time a second property of the Austrian barrel is taught, much more wonderful than the first.

### Definition.

A cylinder and conical frustums are called conjugate when the sections of both through the axis have the same or equal diagonals, and as the diameter of the cylinder's base is to its height, so are the diameters of the frustums' lesser bases to their sloping sides.

### Theorem VI. Problem.

Given a cylinder and the side, or the diameter of the lesser base, of a conjugate frustum, to find the remaining lines of the conjugate frustum. But the ratio of the side or base in the cylinder to the given side or base of the frustum must be less than the ratio of the diameter and height of the cylinder together to the diagonal.

Let there be given the cylinder AGCX, with base diameter CG, height GA and diagonal AC; and let the base diameter CT of the frustum be given; and let the ratio of CG to CT be less than the ratio of CG and GA together to CA. The side of the frustum and the diameter of the greater base must be found. Let it be made, as CG to GA, so CT to some line, say AT; and on CA let a triangle be built from CT and AT. For since the ratio of CG, GA to CA is greater than the ratio of CG to CT, it will also be greater than GA to AT; and joining the terms, the ratio of CG, GA to CA will be greater than that of the same CG, GA to CT, TA. And so CT, TA together will be greater than CA, and a triangle can therefore be made from CT, TA on CA. Let a circle be described about the points C, T, A; and from C let a line equal to []{.page .unprinted n=74}TA be stretched to meet the circle at V, and let AV be joined. I say that TA, CV are the sloping sides of the conjugate frustum and AV the diameter of the greater base.

![](/img/stereometria/p74-1.webp){alt="Schema XXI printed again, as on page 70"}

For when the points T, V are joined, since TA is equal to CV, and the common arc TC is added to the arcs TA and CV, the arcs AC, TV will be equal, and so too the chords AC, TV, and the angle ATC equal to the angle VCT; the quadrilateral ATCV, then, will be regular and inscribed in the circle, with the same diagonal AC or TV as the parallelogram AGCX, and with the same ratio of CT to TA as LG to GA. The frustum whose section through the axis is ATCV and the cylinder whose section is AGCX will therefore be conjugate.

### Theorem VII.

If a cylinder and a conical frustum are conjugate, and the difference of the diameters of the frustum's bases is cut in the ratio of the squares of the cylinder's base diameter and height, the square of that diameter will be equal to the rectangle under the frustum's lesser diameter and the sum of this and the segment corresponding to the cylinder's diameter.

Let AGCX be the section of the cylinder, with diagonal AC; and let the perpendicular from G divide this into the parts CL, LA, so that CL corresponds to CG. Let the section of the frustum ATCV also be formed on the same diagonal AC, by VI above, whose diameters are the lesser CT and the greater AV; and VB, equal to CT, being taken from AV, let the remainder be BA.

Since, then, the quadrilateral ATCV stands in a circle, the square of AC will be equal to the two rectangles under TC, AC and under TA, CV, that is, to []{.page .unprinted n=75}the square of TA or CV, together. Taking the square of AT, then, from the square of AC, there will remain the rectangle under TC, AV, that is, under BV, VA. But also when the square of AG, which is greater than AT, is taken from the same square of AC, there remains the rectangle GC, AX, that is, the square of GC, which is therefore less than the rectangle BVA.

Some rectangle less than BVA, then, will be equal to the square of GC; let it be BVD; the remainder under DA, BV will then be the excess of the rectangle BVA over the square of GC. But this excess is equal to the excess of the square of GA over the square of AT. And since the rectangle BVD was put equal to the square of GC, and the rectangle DBV is the excess of the rectangle BVD over the square of BV or TC, DBV is also the excess of the square of GC over the square of TC. Componendo, then, since the square of AG is to the square of GC as the square of AT to the square of TC, it will also be that as the square of AG to the square of CG, that is, as AL to LC, so is the excess of the square of AG over the square of AT, that is, the rectangle under DA, BV, to the excess of the square of GC over the square of TC, that is, to the rectangle DBV. As AL to LC, then, so is the rectangle under AD, BV to the rectangle DBV; so since the rectangles have the same length BV, as AL to LC, so will their breadths AD to DB be. Turning it back, then: if AB is divided at D so that BD is to DA as CL to LA, that is, as the square of CG to the square of GA, the rectangle DBV will equal the square of GC; which was to be proved.

### Theorem VIII.

In a conjugate cylinder and conical frustum, the ratio of the heights is compounded of the ratio of the diameters of the bases, the lesser of the conical frustum and that of the cylinder, and of the ratio of the perpendicular to the sloping side of the frustum.

In Schema XXI let the conjugate figures be CGAX and CTAV, in which the diameters of the bases are, of the cylinder, CG, XA, of the frustum, the lesser VCT and the greater VA; and let TR be the perpendicular of the frustum and TA its sloping side. I say that the ratio of the heights, GA of the cylinder to TR of the frustum, is compounded of the ratio of GC to CT and of the ratio of AT to TR. The proof of the theorem itself is easy, yet it had to be given separately, because of its corollaries and a notable analogy. For since CG is to GA as CT to TA, alternately GC will be to CT as GA to AT. But the ratio of GA to TR is compounded of the ratio of GA to AT and of the ratio of AT to TR; so also of GC to CT and of AT to TR.

### Corollary and Practice for finding the height.

Given CT and VA, by the previous theorem the square of CG is also given; and given the ratio of this square of CG to the square of GA, there are also given []{.page .unprinted n=77}the square of GA and the square of TA. And the difference of CT and VA is known too, namely BA; and the square of TA is less than the square of TR by a quarter of the square of BA, in every conjugation. For the angles at C and T are equal, and CT, VB equal by hypothesis, and also parallel; so when B and T are joined, BT will be equal to CV; but this is equal to TA by hypothesis; so BTA is isosceles, with TR its perpendicular; so BR is equal to RA, and so the square of BA four times that of RA. Taking a quarter of the square of BA from the square of TA, then, the square of TR, the height of the frustum, remains, which compared with the square of GA makes the ratio sought.

### Consideration of the analogy.

These, then, were some theorems used by the calculation for finding the ratio between a conical frustum and its conjugate cylinder. And from this calculation many things most worthy of consideration are drawn. For first, by Corollaries II and III and the various practices compared with one another, it appears that the ratio of frustum to cylinder is not always the same in any one conjugation, but varies as the ratio of the frustum's diameters varies. And in Corollary II the frustum was found to decrease while the cylinder stayed the same; but in Corollary III the frustum grew, the cylinder again staying the same. For in the lowest line it exceeded the cylinder by less than a hundredth part, higher up by less than a thirty-eighth, then a thirty-third, and so always by a greater part, up to the top line, where the excess was a full tenth. But if the calculation is continued further, the frustum must at last become less than the cylinder again, in the same conjugation. It is asked, then, which frustum in any conjugation is the greatest, and which is equal to the conjugate cylinder. Second, it appeared from the three corollaries and the calculation added that there is some alternation here between conjugations of more than double ratio and conjugations of less. For in the conjugation where the double ratio reigns, in that first of all every frustum seems to be less than the cylinder, though imperceptibly, so that the cylinder itself, as the first of all the frustums, with its ratio of diameters, which is the ratio of equality, is the greatest of all the frustums and equal to itself; but in a conjugation where a ratio greater than []{.page .unprinted n=81}double reigns, all the frustums, even those next to the cylinder itself, become perceptibly less than the conjugate cylinder. Is it then, conversely, that in one where a ratio less than double reigns, the frustums first become greater than the cylinder, up to a certain limit, then decrease again, and become at last equal to their cylinder and then less, until finally the frustums vanish altogether, the cylinder of the conjugation remaining? Third, since the cylinders of all the other conjugations are less than the cylinder of the conjugation using the double ratio, while the frustums of some conjugations grow beyond their conjugate cylinders: it is asked whether this excess is so great as to equal or exceed the cylinder that is greatest over all conjugations; and if so, what then are the ratios of the diameters in which a frustum becomes equal to the greatest cylinder? These things, then, we must work out in the following theorems, as far at least as my knowledge allows; for they are above all necessary to the estimation and comparison of barrels.

### Theorem X.

In every conjugation the frustums, by the increase of the ratio of the diameters, at last become less than any given solid quantity.

Let there be any conjugation, whose ratio is CG to GA. I say that there is a frustum of the same conjugation, such as CTA, namely one in which CT is to TA as CG to GA, less than any proposed solid quantity. This is easy to prove. For the sides CT, TA of the figure can be diminished, the ratio between them always remaining that of CG to GA, until CT and TA together equal the diagonal CA, when the three sides VC, CT, TA together equal the fourth, VA, which is the greatest ratio of the diameters CT to VA there can be in this conjugation; in which case the whole frustum sinks into its base, that is, into the plane of the circle VA. And a surface, however great, is less than any solid quantity.

### Theorem XI.

A cylinder equal to a frustum of equal height has a base made up of a third each of the frustum's two bases and of their mean proportional.

For the rectangle under the frustum's diameters is equal to the square of the mean proportional between the diameters, by Euclid VI, 17; and two such rectangles, together with the square of the difference, make the two squares of the two diameters under which the rectangles are contained, by Euclid II, 7. Three rectangles, then, together with the square of the difference, are equal to three squares, of the two diameters of the bases and of the mean proportional. So one rectangle, with a third of the square of the difference, equals a third of the sum of the proportional squares, and so a third of each of them together. And as the rectangle with the said third is to the squares of the frustum's bases, so is the body of the frustum to the bodies of the cylinders of equal height on the frustum's bases, by XVII of the first part. So also as the said three thirds are to the squares of the bases, so is []{.page .unprinted n=82}the body of the frustum (and so also of the cylinder equal to the frustum) to the bodies of the cylinders of equal height on the frustum's bases. And since the bases of such cylinders are as their bodies, so too will the base of the cylinder equal to the frustum be to the frustum's two bases; so it will be made up of a third each of them and of the mean proportional.

Clavius, in Book V of his *Practical Geometry*, chapter III, uses this theorem somewhat transformed, as I touched on above too; but he uses more difficult principles of proof, not plainly connected with mine; so for clarity's sake I have preferred to use my own principles.

Example. Let the diameters be 19 and 22; multiply each by itself and by each other: the squares will be 361 and 484, and the mean proportional 418. The sum of all is 1263, whose third part, 421, is for the body of the frustum, as 361 is the body of the lesser cylinder.

### Theorem XII.

The base diameter of a cylinder having the same height as a right frustum and the same diagonal is the arithmetic mean between the diameters of the frustum's bases.

Let there be the cylinder CEAS, with diagonal CA, and on it the right frustum CTAV, whose height TR is equal to the height EA or CS of the cylinder. I say that CE, the diameter of the cylinder's base, is the arithmetic mean between CT and AV, the diameters of the frustum's bases. For since the frustum is supposed right, and its sloping sides CV, TA equal, and EA and CS also equal, and the angles at S and E right, being in the section of a cylinder through its axis: VS and TE, sides of the triangles VSC, TEA, will also be equal. And CE is equal to SA. By as much as CE exceeds CT, then, namely by the quantity TE, by so much AV exceeds AS or CE, namely by the equal quantity VS. CE is therefore the arithmetic mean between CT and VA; which was to be proved.

### Theorem XIII.

The excess of a frustum having the same height and the same diagonal as a cylinder has to the cylinder the ratio of a twelfth of the square of the difference to the square of the cylinder's diameter.

Let a cylinder be imagined with base CT and the same height TR as the frustum CTAV: the ratio of this cylinder to the cylinder with base CE and the same height will be that of the square of CT to the square of CE; and as these squares are to their common difference, so are the cylinders CT, CE to the rim that the greater cylinder CE puts round the lesser CT. But the difference of the squares consists of two rectangles under CT, TE, and the square of TE, which is half the difference of CT and AV, namely half of AB, that is, AR. In turn, round the cylinder with base CT is put the coat of the frustum CTAV, and the ratio of this coat to the inscribed cylinder CT is the same as that of the rectangle under AB, BV (or CT) with a third of the square of AB, to the square of CT. And the rectangle under AB, CT is equal to two rectangles under ET, TC, since AB is twice ET. Taking these equals away on both sides, there the square of the greater CE adds to the square of CT the square of []{.page .unprinted n=83}AR, that is, a quarter of the square of AB; but here a third of the same square AD is added. And a third exceeds a quarter by a twelfth: so here one twelfth of the square of AB more is added than there. By so much, then, is the frustum's coat greater than the rim of the cylinder on the base CE. So, the cylinder on the base CT, common to both, being added, the frustum CTAV will be greater than the cylinder CEAS by the said part.

![](/img/stereometria/p83-1.webp){alt="Schema XXI printed a third time, as on page 70"}

Example. Let the frustum have the diameters CT 19 and AV 22. The cylinder CTRS inscribed in the frustum will be, by the foregoing, as 361, the square of 19. And the frustum CTAV will add to it the coat TRA, CSV, 60, the difference AB, 3, being multiplied both by CT, 19, and by its own third part, 1, that is, by 20; and the body of the frustum CTAV will be 421. And the arithmetic mean between 19 and 22 is 20½, which has the square 420¼, for the body of the cylinder CE. For TE, 1½, half the difference AB, 3, multiplied by the lesser TC, 19, twice, makes 57; and the same TE, 1½, by itself makes 2¼. The sum of the rim TE about TC is 59¼; add it to the cylinder CT, 361: the result, as said, is 420¼. The cylinder CEAS, then, is less than the frustum CTAV by three quarters of one, which are the twelfth part of 9, the square of the whole difference AB, 3.

### Corollary and Analogy.

When the difference of the frustum's diameters is increased, the height and the cylinder of equal height on the same diagonal remaining, this excess of the frustum increases too. And the difference AB can be increased until it becomes equal to the arithmetic mean between the diameters, namely when CT vanishes into a point and VA becomes twice the diameter CE of the base of the said cylinder: for then we come to the limit of all frustums, namely to the cone, which has the same diagonal as the side that creates the surface of the cone.

And such a cone, with the same height as the cylinder CE and a base whose diameter is twice, as was said, the diameter of the cylinder CE, is to its cylinder in the ratio 4 : 3. For since the ratio of the diameters is 2 : 1, that of the circles will be 4 : 1, and so too that of cylinders of equal height on those bases, if a cylinder stood on the base of the cone, by Theorems III and XVII of the first part. But the body of the cone, by IV of that part, is a third of this fourfold cylinder, with the same base and height.

[]{.page .unprinted n=84}This, then, is the limit that no frustum reaches. No frustum, I say, adds a third part of its body to a cylinder of equal height that has the arithmetic mean for the diameter of its base.

### Theorem XIV.

A cylinder equal to a frustum and of equal height has a greater diagonal than it.

For by the previous theorem a cylinder having the same or an equal diagonal is less than the frustum if of equal height; so a greater one, namely one equal to a frustum of equal height, has a greater diagonal too.

### Theorem XV.

All the ratios of the frustum's diameters that have place in a conjugation of a certain ratio also have place in a conjugation of greater ratio.

For since in any conjugation CT and TA can be diminished from equality until together they become equal to CA, and AV in turn increased until it equals AC, CV, or AT, TC, CV: in those conjugations in which the ratio of AT to TC is less, that is, AT greater relative to TC, the ratio of the diameters CT, AC can vary more. So every change of ratio that holds where AT is small holds also where AT is greater; but where AT is greater, more hold.

Because of this meeting of various conjugations in the same ratios of diameters, then, the following comparison has its place.

### Theorem XVI.

Every cylinder taller than the greatest, on the same diagonal, has among the cylinders lower than the greatest a companion equal to it, which we shall call its counterpart.

For since the ratio of the bases is reversed from the ratio of the heights, there exist equal cylinders. Look back, then, at Schema XIX, in which let CGA be the greatest cylinder, with base CG and height GA. Let a taller one, CHA, be taken. I say that a lower one is found, such as CBA, whose height BA is to the height HA of the first as the base or square of CH to the square of CB. For where a greater and a lesser are given, an equal is given too. But the square of CH grows from no quantity continually with the lines CQ, CI, CH, CG, CB, CP, up to the square of the diameter CA, making ratios of every kind. In turn, the height, starting from the length of the diameter AC, decreases with the lines AQ, AI, AH, AG, AB, AP, likewise through all ratios, so that a ratio greater than any given ratio comes about, until the height is used up []{.page .unprinted n=85}in the point A. So where the decreases of the heights AB fall headlong through all ratios, the increases of the ratios growing to infinity, there the increases of the squares CB become less and less, and the increases of the ratios decrease. And since there is a cylinder lower than CHA and also greater than it, such as CGA, and so with a smaller ratio of HA to GA than of the square of CG to CH, then going down towards B the passage will be through some point where the ratio of BA to HA, which before was less (since it grows faster), becomes equal to the ratio of the square of BC to the square of HC, which grows more slowly, though before it was greater. Examples of equal cylinders are in the calculation at Theorems IV and V.

### Theorem XVII.

In any one conjugation whose square of the diameter is less than twice the square of the height, all the frustums next to the cylinder itself gradually become greater than the conjugate cylinder, even though their height decreases; afterwards they decrease again, but are always still greater than the conjugate cylinder so long as they have a height greater than the counterpart of the conjugate cylinder.

In Schema XIX let there be a conjugation in which the square of the diameter HC is less than twice the square of HA, and the cylinder of this conjugation CHA; and let its companion, that is, equal in body, from another conjugation, be the cylinder CBA, with base CB and height BA. I say first that all the frustums of the conjugation CHA whose height lies between HA and BA are greater than the cylinders CHA or CBA. For between the equal companions CHA, CBA lies the greatest cylinder CGA, in which the square of CG is twice the square of GA. All the cylinders CGA, then, between GHA and CBA, are greater than the extremes ending at H and B, by Theorem V of this part; and they have heights between HA and BA, as do the frustums. And by XIII of this part frustums of equal height to cylinders on the same diagonal CA are greater than they. So they are much greater than the lesser extremes ending at I and B.

![](/img/stereometria/p85-1.webp){alt="Schema XIX, printed again with its caption, as on page 63"}

### []{.page .unprinted n=86}Theorem XVIII.

In a conjugation of less than double ratio, the frustum equal to the conjugate cylinder has a height less than the height of the cylinder that is the companion of the conjugate cylinder and equal to it, but of a different conjugation.

For a frustum of equal height to such a companion cylinder, which is equal to the conjugate one, is greater than it, by XIII; so it is also greater than its conjugate cylinder. One that is equal in body to its conjugate, then, will not be of the same height as that one. So it is either taller or lower. But not taller, by XVII above; so lower. And from there, by X of this part, the frustums become less than the conjugate cylinder, until at last they vanish.

### Corollary.

Supposing barrels to consist of a pure doubled conical frustum: oblong barrels with moderate bellies are more capacious than cylindrical ones of the same length of figure without a belly; but it never happens that they have bellies so huge and extravagant as to make them in turn less capacious than cylindrical barrels of the same length of figure.

### Theorem XIX.

In all conjugations of frustums and cylinder in which the diameter of the lesser base is less than √2 times the sloping side, there is twice some ratio of the frustum's diameters by which the frustum becomes equal to the cylinder that is greatest over all conjugations.

Let GC, the diameter of the cylinder's base, be to GA, its height (in this theorem, that is), less than √2 : 1. I say that in this conjugation two ratios of the diameters CT, AV occur by which a frustum can be made equal to the greatest cylinder, whose base diameter is √2 times its height. For if GC is less than √2 times GA, GA is greater than CA divided by √3: so some line less than AG can be taken, AT, and TC compared with AT in the ratio of AG to GC, so that the perpendicular from T, TR, is CA divided by √3; and the frustum ATCV will be of equal height to the greatest cylinder, whose diameter is √2 times its height. Such a frustum will then still be greater than this greatest cylinder, by Theorem XIII of this part. And since, by XIV of this part, the cylinder to be equalled, if of equal height to the frustum, has a greater diagonal, one having a smaller diagonal, namely the same as the frustum that is to be equal, must have a greater height than the frustum, to recover by the increase of height what it lost by the shortening of the []{.page .unprinted n=87}diagonal. That is, the frustum that is to be equal to it must become less than the one of equal height to the greatest cylinder built on the same diagonal. And this can happen in two ways. For since in any one conjugation the cylinder is the beginning of all the frustums, and in the conjugations proposed here the cylinder is less than the greatest cylinder, the frustums next to their conjugate cylinder, and so taller, will also be less than the greatest cylinder. But from there, by the increase of AB, the difference of the diameters CT, AV, they grow until they become greater than the greatest cylinder, as has now been proved. In this increase of AB, then, while TR is less than the height GA of its conjugate cylinder and greater than the height of the greatest cylinder, it happens once that the frustum becomes equal to the greatest cylinder. But the frustums of the same conjugation do not grow without end as AB grows; by X and XVIII of this part they decrease again; and in this variation it happens a second time that the frustum becomes equal to the greatest cylinder. And since the one of equal height to the greatest (whose TR is AC divided by √3) was greater than that greatest, once made lower than the greatest cylinder it will at some point become equal to it again. TA must therefore be diminished further. And when TA is diminished, and TC with it proportionally, the square of TA is diminished; but the square of CA remains; so the remaining rectangle TC, AV is increased; but its breadth TC is diminished, as said; so in turn, and on two counts, the length AV of the rectangle is increased; and in this way a certain ratio of TC to AV is set up, by using which the frustum becomes equal to the greatest cylinder a second time. Which was to be proved.

### Theorem XX.

Frustums of various conjugations having the same ratio of diameters among themselves will be the greater the nearer they come in height to the greatest cylinder on the same diagonal, and the less the taller they are than it.

This too is unexpected. Who would not say that a frustum whose height is IA is greater than another frustum whose height is GA, if both have the same diagonal GA and the same ratio of the lesser bases to the greater? Yet the truth is otherwise. For let there be the greatest cylinder, whose base diameter CG is to its height GA as √2 : 1, by Theorem V; and let there be a frustum whose lesser diameter is in the line CG and greater in AX (extended), and let their ratio be any whatever, and GC the arithmetic mean between them, by Theorem XII. Since, then, the same ratio of the two bases to each other is supposed through the different frustums, the ratio of their difference to the arithmetic mean will also be the same through them all. And since every such frustum exceeds the cylinder of equal height in the ratio of a twelfth of the square of the difference of the frustum's diameters to the square of their arithmetic mean, by Theorem XIII of this part, this excess of the frustum will be greater where the cylinder is greater. But the cylinder CGA is the greatest of all []{.page .unprinted n=88}on the same diagonal; so the excess of this frustum, or the said twelfth, will be the greatest of all the others through the other frustums having the same ratio of diameters. And therefore, although the height of the frustum is greater than GA, yet because the cylinder of this height is less, the frustum too will be less.

For example, let the greatest ratio of a frustum's diameters of all be taken, that is, an infinite one; let a cone, I say, be taken for the frustum, the end of all frustums. Let the cone's height be IA, whose square is half the square of AC. And let the base of the cylinder CIA of equal height be the line CI, equal to the height AI. And since CI is the arithmetic mean between the frustum's diameters, and one of them is 0, the other, namely the diameter of the cone's base, will be twice CI. Here, then, for the body of this cone a third of AI must be multiplied by the square of twice CI, which is four times the square of CI, and so twice the square of CA.

![](/img/stereometria/p88-1.webp){alt="Schema XXI printed a fourth time, as on page 70"}

But another cone (again taken for a frustum with the same ratio of diameters), with height GA, whose square is a third of the square of CA, will likewise have a base diameter twice GC, and so a square four times as great. And the square of GC is two thirds of the square of AC; so four two-thirds are eight thirds, which exceed the double, or six thirds, of the first cone by two thirds; whereas, on the other hand, the square of the first one's height, having half the square of CA, exceeds the square of this one's height, which is a third of the square of CA, by only a sixth of the square of CA. Nor yet must all of this loss be counted: because it is not the squares but the heights themselves that are multiplied by the bases, and indeed not even the heights themselves, but only their thirds. The gain of the cone in the longer base CG, then, is greater than the loss in the shorter height GA.

### Theorem XXI.

Of all the frustums of the same conjugation the greatest is the one that has the height of the greatest cylinder, []{.page .unprinted n=89}namely the diagonal divided by √3; and from this peak all the others, both taller and lower, decrease again.

Let Snellius or Adriaan van Roomen, the Apolloniuses of the Low Countries, work out a lawful proof, or a refutation if need be. This is indeed a by-work here, and an addendum as far as the aim of the little book goes; yet it is written in here for kinship's sake with the previous theorem, whose likeness to this gave me my first belief in it: although the frustums of the previous theorem are not conjugate in the sense in which we have so far used that word, yet they too are described on the same diagonal and have the same ratio of bases among themselves, just as the other, conjugate ones have the same ratio of the diameter of the lesser base to the sloping side.

A second showing comes from the property of the conjugation of the greatest cylinder, and from what I proved above: that there is some height of a conjugate frustum greater than the height of the greatest cylinder, and some less, the frustums of both of which are equal to the greatest cylinder; but that the frustum of equal height to the greatest cylinder itself has a greater body; and that on both sides, both towards the conjugate cylinder and towards the lowest frustums, the frustums decrease. No reason appears, then, why the limit of the increases of the body should lie anywhere else in the neighbourhood than here.

Add the evidence of calculation. Let there be a conjugation in which the diameter CI equals the height IA, and let there be a frustum of this conjugation of equal height to the cylinder CGA, here the greatest, namely the one whose diameter CG is √2 times its height GA. The frustum's lesser diameter, then, will be in CG, and it is equal to the sloping side, since it is supposed to be to it as CI to IA. So CF is the lesser diameter, cutting IN, the perpendicular from the centre N, at the point F, and FA is the sloping side. As CL to CG, then, so CN to CF. But CL is two thirds of GA, and the square of CL four ninths of the square of CA; and the square of CN is a quarter of the square of CA; and finally the square of CG is two thirds of the square of CA. And as CL, four ninths, is to CG, two thirds, so is CN, a quarter, to CF, three eighths. So the square of CF will be three eighths of the square of CA; and the square of the height FA is as much. Three eighths, then, taken from the square of CA, leave five eighths for the rectangle under CF, the lesser diameter, and the opposite, greater diameter, which coincides with AX, running out beyond X. Since, then, the square of CF and the rectangle of the diameters CF and the greater have the same breadth CF, their lengths will be as the planes themselves, namely as three to five. This, then, is the ratio of the diameters of the frustum of this conjugation that is of equal height to the greatest cylinder. So, by XIII of this part, as the square 16 of their arithmetic mean 4, namely of CG, is to a twelfth of the square 4 of the difference 2 of the diameters (and a twelfth of 4 is a third), so is the body of the greatest cylinder CGA to the excess of the frustum CFA of equal height: the frustum, then, exceeds the greatest cylinder by a third of a sixteenth, or a forty-eighth part of the body of the cylinder CGA. Let the ratio of the greatest cylinder CGA to the conjugate cylinder CIA be sought, then. The square of the base CI is half the square of CA, and the square of CG is two thirds of the square of CA: so the ratio of the bases CI to CA is as 3 to 4. In turn, the square of the height IA is a half, []{.page .unprinted n=90}and the square of the height GA a third, of the square of CA: so the ratio of the squares IA to GA is that of 3 to 2, or 9 to 6; and so the ratio of the lines IA to GA themselves is its square root, namely that of 30000 to 24495−, or of this to 20000. And the ratio of bodies is compounded of the ratio of the base and the ratio of the heights. Multiplying the corresponding terms together, then, the ratio of the cylinder CIA to the cylinder CGA comes out the same as that of 9000 to 9798−. Above, at Theorem III, the ratio of the columns of these conjugations was that of 2828− to 3080−. And if I change this number of the cylinder CGA into 16, or 48 thirds, the number of the first, in this ratio, will be 44 for the cylinder CIA. So the cylinder CIA is to its conjugate frustum CFA as 44 to 49, and the frustum greater by a little more than a ninth part. And this agrees with this theorem and with Corollary III to Theorem IX of this part; for there, when the ratio of the diameters was that of 2 to 3, that is, of 20 to 30, the frustum exceeded by a little more than an eleventh part, and was growing; so it grew until the ratio was that of 3 to 5, that is, 18 to 30, when the frustum exceeded most, by more than a ninth part, as is proved here. Further on, when the ratio was that of 1 to 2, that is, 15 to 30, the frustum had decreased again, so as to exceed by only a ninth part. What happens in one conjugation, then, that the frustum of equal height to the greatest cylinder grows most beyond the conjugate cylinder, it is reasonable to think happens in all.

### Theorem XXII.

In conjugations that have the square of the diameter twice the square of the height or more, all the frustums are less than the greatest cylinder, which is their conjugate; and the more so the further we depart from the double ratio.

In Schema XIX let CGA be the conjugation of the double ratio of the square of CG to the square of GA, and let CA be one of lesser ratio, with counterpart cylinders at H and B: all the cylinders between H and B, then, and so also the frustums of equal height to them, are greater than the cylinder ending at H or B; and a little below B, down to A, all the frustums are less than the cylinders H and B, by XV and XVI above.

And the nearer H is to G, the nearer too will B be to G, and the smaller the arc HB in which the frustums are greater; at last, then, the cylinder CGA is the same, itself and its own counterpart, standing in place of both H and B. Just as, then, all the cylinders after B are less than I itself, and all the frustums a little below B, so here too all the cylinders after G, and with them the frustums of equal height, will be less than the conjugate CGA itself. For the reason why there the frustums begin to become less not at B but a little below B is that at B itself there is a frustum of the conjugation CIA of equal height to the cylinder. But here at G itself there is no frustum of the conjugation CGA of equal height to the cylinder; the first frustums from the cylinder CGA, then, are at once lower than the line GA, losing more by height than they gain by the addition of the third.

The same analogy also appears thus. For the frustums of this conjugation, all those from the cylinder itself, are lower than it, since it is the head of its conjugation. And since it is itself the greatest of the cylinders, all the frustums conjugate to it are less, by Theorem XXI, in the order in which they are distant from its height: the greatest among them, then, is the cylinder itself, as alone among them equal in height to itself.

[]{.page .unprinted n=91}This is a proof by analogy that cannot be refuted; but since geometers have not much accustomed themselves to analogies, come, let us try a more laborious and plainly geometrical proof, calling back Schema XXI. In it let there be the frustum CTAV of the conjugation CGA; and let CT be extended until it cuts the circle at E, and let the points E, A be joined. The cylinder CEA, then, is less than the cylinder CGA, by Theorem V. And the frustum CTAV is of equal height to it, by construction, since EA and TR are equal. So the frustum is greater than the cylinder CEA by a third of the square of AR, by XIII above. It must be proved that the ratio of GA to AE is greater than the ratio of the square of CE with a third of the square of AR or ET to the square of CG, so that the cylinder CEA has lost more by the lowness of EA than it gained by the third of the square of ET. First, then, the square of TR or AE is less than the square of TA by the whole square of AR.

![](/img/stereometria/p91-1.webp){alt="Schema XXI printed a fifth time, as on page 70"}

And the square of CE is less than the square of GE with a third of the square of AR by that third of the square of AR. The difference between the terms there, then, is made by a quantity that is three times the quantity that makes the terms differ here. And besides, these terms are greater than twice those terms. For since the square of CG is twice the square of AG, here the square of CE is greater than the square of CG, and the square of AE less than the square of GA. And when between some terms, such as 25 and 26, some quantity, such as 1, makes the difference, and three times that quantity, such as 3, makes the difference between terms less than their halves, or at least the second less than half the second, as between 10 and 13, the ratio of the lesser terms is greater than six times the ratio of the greater: as 10 to 13, or 20 to 26, has a ratio greater than the sixth power of the ratio 25 to 26; and it would be much greater if the second term 13 too had been less than half the second, 26. For with an equal difference remaining, the smaller the terms become, the more the ratio increases. The ratio of the square of TA to the square of TR or EA, then, is greater than the sixth power of the ratio of the squares of CE and a third of AR to CE. []{.page .unprinted n=92}So the ratio of the lines TA to TR or EA themselves is greater than the cube of that of the squares of CE and a third of AR to the square of CE.

In the same way we shall also prove that the square of CE has a smaller ratio to the square of CG than the straight lines GA to AT. For the square of CG is equal to the rectangle BVD, BA being divided at D so that AD is half of DB, as the square of AG is half that of GC, by Theorem VII of this part. But CE is the arithmetic mean between VB and VA, by Theorem XII of this part. So the square of CE equals the whole rectangle BVA and besides the square of AR, half of AB. If, then, a parallelogram equal to the square of AR is applied to BV or CT, the breadth of the parallelogram, AH, will be added to VA. The square of CE, then, will be equal to the rectangle BVH. And the square of CG was equal to the rectangle BVD. So as HV to VD, so is the square of EC to the square of CG. I say that the ratio of HV to VD is less than that of DV to VB. For BD is twice DA, and BA twice AR; so AD too is twice DR; and if AH were equal to AD, and so HD equal to DB, HV to VD would still be less than DV to VB. But AH is less than AD, and so also less than DR. For the square of CT or VB is always greater than twice BA, by Theorem X of this part. For if it were twice, the height of the frustum would be nothing and its body nothing; the square of VB, then, is always greater than eight times the square of AR. Since, then, VB, AR, AH are in continued proportion, the line VB itself will also always be greater than eight times AH, and so AR the mean proportional between AH, 1, and BV, 8+. Of such parts, then, as AR has the root of 8+, AH has 1. But the root of 8 lies between 2 and 3, at a frustum height of nothing and a body of nothing; and it quickly happens, even at the least height of the frustum, that 8 becomes 9, when its root is 3, and at greater heights always greater. So AH, where greatest, lies between a half and a third of AR, and by the increase of the height of the conjugate frustums becomes less than any part of AR, so that at last, with AB itself vanishing (the frustum passing into a mere cylinder), it becomes an infinitely small portion of AR. But if AH were not there, the ratio of AV to VD would be less than half that of DV to VB, because AD is half of DB; and since the ratio of DV to VB is the square of that of GC to CT, or GA to AT: so if AH were not there, the ratio of AV to VD, and so also of the square of EC to the square of CG, would always be in a smaller ratio than the straight lines GA to AT.

But since between AR and AH, through different frustums, there are ratios of every kind, it happens somewhere, namely in frustums of very nearly equal height to the cylinder, that AH adds only so much that the ratio of HV to VA equals the ratio of GA to AT. And then the matter is certain: for the two elements of the ratio of GA to AE, the one AT to EA or TR, the other GA to AT, are each greater than the two elements of the ratio of the square of GE with a third of the square of AR to the square of GC.

In the frustums next to the conjugate cylinder, then, the height has decreased in a greater ratio than that in which the base of the cylinder equal to the frustum has grown; and this alone had to be proved: for once the beginning of the frustums is given as less than the cylinder, the frustums go on decreasing continually until they vanish. The following []{.page .unprinted n=93}cases, however, belong to the greater clearness of the proof.

Either the ratio of HV to VD is equal to the ratio of GA to AT, in lower frustums; and so the second elements of the said ratios are equal, but the first still unequal, and the excess of the line AT over TR, compared with TR, more than three times greater than the third of the square of AR compared with the square of GE, and that without compensation; the whole ratio of the heights, then, is still greater than the ratio of the bases.

Or, finally, the ratio of HV to VB exceeds the ratio of GA to AT, but in still lower frustums, when CE is made long and TR short; where the square of AR becomes more and more equal to the square of TR, and at last exceeds it, making a great ratio between TR and TA; which indeed from the very beginning, and always, is greater than three times the ratio between the square of CE with a third of the square of AR and the square of CE alone; whereas, on the other hand, the third of the square of RA does not increase the square of CE in the same increase of ratio, since that itself grows on its own. And since it soon happens that the element of the ratio of the heights, which is the ratio of AT to TR, becomes greater than a third part of the ratio of GA to AT, from then on it always happens that the excess of this element in the ratio of the heights not only compensates for, but more and more exceeds, the excess of that element in the ratio of the bases. For here the whole growing square of AR increases the ratio of lesser and decreasing terms; there only a third, growing evenly, increases the ratio of greater and growing terms. A third part of the increase of the ratio, then, can do more here than the whole increase of the ratio there.

These things, then, had to be proved of the conjugation in which the square of CG is twice the square of GA. And if the square of CG is more than twice it, all this holds much more. For as regards the analogy, the cylinders of equal height to such frustums (in Schema XIX, CBA) are the counterparts of the equal but taller cylinders CHA, of which it was proved in Theorem XVIII that the frustums begin to become less from them, even those that have the tall cylinders CHA as their conjugates: how much more those that are of the same conjugation as their counterparts CBA, and which arise only here, made lower than the conjugate cylinder CBA at their first rising.

Now as regards the rest of the proof: since in Schema XXI the cylinders themselves, the heads of the conjugations, are supposed lower than the cylinder GA, CG grows in them, but by decreasing increases, and GA decreases, but by increasing decreases. And when GA is diminished, and TA in accordance with it, AB too, the difference of the diameters, though these grow, grows, but by decreasing increases, by Theorem X of this part. The reduction of the body of the conjugate cylinder by the shortening of TR, then, is great, both because of the great base CG and because of the much greater difference of TR and AG; but the addition to the body of the conjugate cylinder, by the third of the square of AR and the increase of the square of CE by BA, is small and of less account. Let anyone who wishes unravel all the elements of the foregoing proof in this way; he will find no less light than shone out from the calculation above at Theorem IX of this part, on the truth of this part.

### []{.page .unprinted n=94}Corollary.

Supposing that wine barrels consist of pure conical frustums: in short ones, indeed, every belly lessens the estimate of capacity; but in Austrian ones it is almost the same whether they have a belly or imitate the cylindrical shape more closely: because it never happens that the belly grows to such a width as to have a depth twice the diameter of the wooden head, in which case indeed, by the corollaries to Theorem IX, it would lose more than a quarter. Nor does the depth ever become one and a half times the diameter of the wooden head, in which case the belly would lose about a thirtieth part. And if four thirds, which is very unusual, the loss from the belly is already reduced to a seventieth part.

And this is that second, and indeed most noble, property of the Austrian barrel. For just as by Theorem V nothing much could harm it when its shape was varied somewhat by the cooper's error, because, with law and custom as his guides, the craftsman aimed at the most capacious shape of all and hit upon shapes next to the most capacious, in which, being next to it, the shortfall, by the laws of the circle, cannot be observed at first: so now too it makes almost no difference in this barrel whether the belly is wide or narrow, a thing above all welcome to the craftsman; because it is not so easy to give the belly the width one pleases as it is to give the proportion of the staves to the heads; nor does he foresee exactly how great the barrel's belly will turn out, and he would be put in a tight spot if a law prescribed a fixed width of belly. That there is no need of such a troublesome law, then, must be credited to the convenience of the proportion of staves to heads that Austria observes.

### Theorem XXIII. A problem put to geometers.

Given the ratio of a frustum's diameters, to find the conjugation in which such a frustum equals the cylinder of the greatest conjugation.

First, in the conjugation of the greatest cylinder itself every frustum, and so also the frustum with the given ratio of diameters, is already less than the greatest cylinder, by Theorem XXI of this part. So the conjugation sought is above G, the conjugation of the greatest cylinder, towards the conjugation of the frustum of equal height to the greatest that has the given ratio of diameters. As, if the frustum of equal height to the greatest cylinder CGA is CFA, and its conjugate cylinder CIA, and there is also in the conjugation CGA the frustum CTA, and as CF is to the opposite parallel diameter through AX, so is CT to AV, the body of the frustum CTA of the conjugation G will be less than the greatest cylinder CGA. The conjugation sought, then, will fall above the conjugation G, towards I. So the frustum sought will have a height greater than GA. And the base diameter of the cylinder of equal height, []{.page .unprinted n=95}which is the arithmetic mean between the diameters of the frustum sought, will have a length less than CG. I say that the conjugation sought is even beyond I, and the height of the frustum sought greater than AI, which may seem strange. For since the ratio of the frustum's diameters is given, their ratio to their arithmetic mean is always given too. As CF to CG, then, so will the lesser diameter of the frustum sought be to the diameter of its cylinder of equal height. But this is less than CG, and further from A: so that too is less than CF, and further from A: so the ratio of CF to FA, or CI to IA, is less than that of the diameter of the frustum sought to its sloping side, that is, of the diameter of the conjugate cylinder to its height. So the height of the conjugation sought is greater than AI, and its diameter less than IC. So far the proof: the rest of this problem I hand over to Adriaan van Roomen to work out, and to anyone else who has a liking for Geber.

For since every frustum is greater than its cylinder of equal height in the ratio of a twelfth of the square of the difference of the diameters to the square of the diameter of the cylinder of equal height, we are told to divide the square of CA in such a way that one part, increased by a portion given by the difference of the diameters and multiplied by the side of the other part, equals the square of CG multiplied by the line GA. Consulted on this, Geber answered from his algebra that a height must be found so great that, with three quantities in continued proportion after it, in the ratio of it to CA, a certain multiple of the first equals a given absolute number together with a certain multiple of the third: and algebraists are still seeking such equations in geometry, and, in my judgement, will never find them.

### Theorem XXIV. A problem put to geometers.

Given a conjugation in which the square of the diameter of the cylinder's base is less than twice the square of the height, to find the two ratios of the diameters that make frustums of the same conjugation equal to the greatest cylinder.

Let the conjugation CIA be given, in which the square of CI is less than twice the square of IA: the diameters of the conjugate frustums equal to the greatest cylinder CGA must be found. The height of one frustum, then, will be greater than GA, the height of the greatest cylinder, and that of the other less, by XXI of this part. Consequently, the ratio of the diameters of the former will be less than the ratio of the diameters of the frustum of equal height to the greatest cylinder CAG, and that of the latter greater: each will have its cylinder of equal height; and these will not be counterparts of each other, but next to counterparts: because if they were counterparts, they would be equal, by XVI of this part. But since their frustums have unequal ratios of diameters, having different heights in the same conjugation, they would add unequal twelfths of the squares of their differences, by XIII of this part; so the frustums themselves would become unequal. But we require them equal, each being equal to the one CGA.

[]{.page .unprinted n=96}So far the proof: let the algebraists finish the rest. For let CF, which is sought, be given: FA too, then, is given from the conjugation, and its square; and so also the rectangle of the diameters, and, this being divided by the given lesser diameter CF, the greater diameter too; and so the difference of the greater and the lesser will be known, and a twelfth and a quarter of its square; and when the quarter is subtracted from the square of AF, the square of the height will remain, which compared with the square of GA will show one part of the ratio of the bodies. So, both diameters being given, the base diameter, that is, its square, of the cylinder that has the height of the frustum CFA is given, by XII of this part; and the twelfth found before, added to it, makes a sum which compared with the square of CG will show the other part of the ratio of the bodies. And these parts of the ratio of the bodies must be inversely equal, so that combined into one they make the ratio of equality.

Take up, algebraists, the cross of the mind that I have set up, and follow me: you will find, unless Minerva has looked on me with averted face, that the first, second and fifth of continued proportionals, each taken a certain number of times, are equal to an absolute number together with the third and fourth of the proportionals. So the equation is not geometrical, but the trial-and-error one of Nicolaus Reimers Ursus, or the mechanical one of Jost Bürgi; nor is the problem of the kind Pappus, after the custom of the ancients, called plane, that is, absolutely geometrical and scientific, but solid, and geometrical only on a condition, namely given two means in continued proportion, which has no scientific solution. And besides, this equation has more than one solution; for it has been proved that there are two such frustums.

### Theorem XXV.

If frustums of different conjugations have the same ratio of diameters among themselves and are set up on the same diagonal, the ratio of their bodies will be compounded of three elements: of the ratio of the cylinders of the conjugations, and of the ratios of each cylinder to its conjugate frustum, that of the first cylinder reversed and that of the second direct.

Let there be the frustum CFA of the conjugation CIA and the frustum CTA of the conjugation CGA, on the same diagonal CA; and let CF be to the greater diameter extended through AX as CT to AV. I say that the ratio of the body CFA to the body of the frustum CTA is compounded of three ratios: 1. CIA to CGA; 2. CFA to CIA; 3. CGA to CTA. It is a simple application of the most familiar axiom, that when four quantities are set in order, the ratio of the first to the fourth is compounded of the ratios in between: only care must be taken in setting them out: for since we are concerned with the ratio of the frustums to each other, one of the frustums must be set in the fourth place and the other in the first, with the cylinders in the middle, each next to its conjugate. For by the corollary to Theorem III the ratio of the columns, and []{.page .unprinted n=97}so too of the cylinders, is given, namely CIA to CGA. And by Theorem IX the reversed ratio of CIA to CFA, namely CFA to CIA, is given, and the direct ratio of CGA to CTA: and when the bodies of given ratio are set out in turn, the said series results.

### Corollary and Practice.

The terms by which the three ratios are expressed being rightly set out, multiply the three antecedents, two together and the product by the third; do the same with the three consequents; and the products will contain the ratio sought.

### On the cubic rod and its reliability.

### Theorem XXVI.

In barrels of similar shape to one another, the ratio of the capacities is the cube of the ratio of those lengths that run from the top of the bung-hole to the lowest edge of either wooden head.

Let there be barrels of different size and the same kind, SQKT and XGCZ, whose bung-holes are O and A, the diameters of their wooden heads QK, ST and GC, XZ, and their lowest points T, K and Z, C; and let the lengths OK, OT be equal, and likewise AC, AZ. I say that the capacities of the barrels are in the cubed ratio of the lengths OK and AC. For through O and A let planes OV, AY be drawn parallel to the wooden heads, and let there be two conical frustums SV and VQ, and likewise XY and YG, similar to each other.

![](/img/stereometria/p98-1.webp){alt="Schema XXII: a small barrel SQKT and a large barrel XGCZ drawn side by side, each with the gauging rod laid from the bung O or A to the foot of both heads, among clover and flowers"}

What is true of the ratio of half barrels, then, will be true of whole ones too. Let the proposed figures be the conical frustums OVKQ, AYCG, and let the sides of the figures be OQ, VK and AG, YC; the diameters of the lesser bases QK, GC; the diameters of the greater bases OV, AY; and OQKV, AGCY the quadrilateral sections of the figures through their axes, similar to each other, and their diagonals OK, AC.

Since, then, similar figures are to each other in the cubed ratio of corresponding sides, the ratio of the body GY to the body QV will be the cube of the ratio of the side AG to the side OQ, or of the diameter GC to the diameter QK. But in the similar plane triangles AGC and OQK, as GC is to the corresponding QK, or AG to the corresponding OQ, so too is the diagonal AC to the corresponding []{.page .unprinted n=99}diagonal OX. So the ratio of the body GY to the body QV is also the cube of the ratio of the length AC to the length OK; and so too of the whole barrel GZ to the whole barrel QT.

### Corollary I, and the making of the rod.

It is plain from this that if the gauging rod is divided into equal parts so large that the first and lowest of them is the length OK of a small barrel holding one measure, and to each of the equal parts numbers are added which are to one another in the cubed ratio of the numbers of the even division, namely 1 at the end of the first part, 8 at the end of two parts, 27 at 3, 64 at 4, 125 at 5, and so on, and the other numbers that fall between these cubes are arranged in the spaces between, as the second part is subdivided into 7 more little parts, not equal but proportional, to which the numbers 2, 3, 4, 5, 6, 7, between 1 and 8, can be set, and so with the rest: then, when the rod is rightly put into the barrel, the numbers up to which the inner surface of the stave at OA reaches, the start of the rod standing at K, C or T, Z, are indexes of the measures the barrel holds, that is, of the ratio which the barrel, GZ for example, has to the barrel QT of one measure.

### Corollary II.

It comes to the same thing that some, in the triangle AGC, measure in place of the side AC the sum of the sides AG and GC, carrying instead of a rod a rolled strip of parchment inscribed by the same law with the numbers of measures. They fix the start of it, unrolled, at the edge C, stretch its length from C to G and on to A, and pronounce the number of measures from the mark that touches the point A. For they presuppose that the length of the margin running out at G, and the thickness of the wooden hoops bound with withies, and of the staves and the wooden head GC, which they take in by carrying the strip round, are alike in all barrels.

### Theorem XXVII.

Even if the two halves of an Austrian barrel are not quite alike, but one of the wooden heads is a little smaller and narrower than the other, provided the length on the gauging rod is the same, the difference of capacities in the two halves will be imperceptible.

For it was said in the corollary to Theorem V of the second part that the Austrian barrel is about the most capacious shape, from which all shapes on either side, that is, barrels both longer and shorter than the Austrian, are all less capacious than the Austrian. []{.page .unprinted n=100}And at those points at which the change goes from less to greatest and again to less, by some law of the circle, that difference is always imperceptible for some way. What is true of whole barrels having the same diagonal, then, will also be true of the two frustums of one barrel, as of AYX and AVG: so that even if one of the heads, say GC, is smaller than the other, XZ, provided AC and AZ are equal, the capacity on both sides is equal to every perception.

### Theorem XXVIII.

But if the length of the rod through the two frustums of the barrel is not equal, as happens, the mean proportional between the two lengths of the rod, that is, the middle number between the two marked from the one half and the other, is used without error as the index of capacity.

For the lesser length has inscribed on it twice the capacity of its own frustum, and the greater likewise has inscribed twice that of its own frustum. The two lengths, then, with their numbers added, have inscribed twice the capacity of the whole barrel. So the arithmetic mean between the numbers of the two lengths, which is equivalent to the mean proportional between the lines, shows the single measure of the whole barrel.

### Theorem XXIX.

The curvature of the staves, or bulge between the middle bung-hole and each wooden head, takes nothing from the rod's reading in an Austrian barrel; in oblong barrels it increases the capacity shown by the rod (of itself, that is, other things being equal), in short ones it lessens it.

For although, by XXIX of the first part, a barrel in the shape of a cut lemon, plum, olive, parabolic or hyperbolic spindle exceeds in capacity a cylindrical barrel, or one having the shape of a mere doubled frustum, by these degrees, in the order in which the shapes are listed here, yet that is of itself very little, as appears from Theorem XXII of the first part; and whatever of it is perceptible is already brought into the rod's numbers. For the first barrel, whose capacity was inscribed on the rod as the mark of one measure, had its bulge like all the others: so the rod shows all barrels as of a like bulge; which, though it is not alike in all Austrian barrels, is yet some in all, and so the error about it is less. But when barrels are longer than Austrian ones, the bend of the staves from the bung-hole to the heads is also longer in them, and so of greater capacity, even if the bend is supposed alike on both sides; just as, if they are shorter than Austrian ones, this bend is shorter. But the rod shows a bend of moderate length, such as is in the Austrian barrel: so the rod (other things being equal) does not reach the length of the bend in an oblong barrel, and goes beyond it in a short one.
