## []{.page .unprinted n=9}The Solid Geometry of Barrels. Preamble on the reason for the shape of the wine barrel.

Every skilful and convenient measurement of a space requires also a regular shape; for vessels of no definite and no regular shape refuse the intellect, and wait only on the hand and on counting, one after another, the measures of liquid poured in.

Wine vessels have come by a round shape, akin to the cone and the cylinder, from the needs of their material, their construction and their use. For liquid kept long in metal vessels is spoiled by rust; vessels of glass and earthenware are neither to be had in quantity nor safe; vessels of stone are unfit for use because of their weight. It remains, then, that we receive and store wine in wooden ones. But again, vessels cannot easily be made from a single trunk, nor large ones, nor in the numbers needed; and if they could, they would split. Casks must therefore be built from many pieces of wood fitted together. But the joints of the wood cannot be secured against the liquid running out by any material or any craft, except only by the tightening of hoops. And since the hoops are of a pliant material, of birch, oak or the like, under the pressure of the yielding mass of the things they bind with some force they spread out into the most capacious figure of all. With the best of reason, then, coopers are brought to circles: lest, if the belly of the cask strives, as has been said, after a circular width, they should give the outer ends some other shape and make a crooked and weak vessel. This can be seen in the flasks in which Italian wines are carried over the Alps into Germany. Since these are of a flattened shape, as their use requires, so that they can be hung at the sides of mules and carried without harm through narrow roads, and so that, by standing out too far from the mules' sides, they should not by the law of the balance weigh more on the beasts and make the jolting more violent: on the side where they are flatter they also bear a blow more weakly and easily burst open.

There is also the convenience of the circular or cylindrical shape that, since wines must be carried overland on wagons, the load should have the most wine and the least wood. On this account, if a globe could be fitted together from wooden boards, vessels would above all have been globular. But because a globe cannot be bound with hoops, the cylinder took the globe's place. Nor yet could this be a pure and simple cylinder: for the hoops, once loosened, would at once be useless, and could not be driven on more forcibly, unless the cask narrowed somewhat on both sides from the middle of the belly in a conical shape. This shape is also suited to rolling straight on (whence the cylinder's name) and to carrying on wagons; and with its base doubled it is put together into a kind of likeness of itself, most convenient for balance and handsome to look at.

Since, then, wine casks share in the circle, the cone and the cylinder, regular figures, they are so far suited to geometrical measurement: and it is worth the trouble to set the principles of this in the entrance hall of this inquiry, as they were investigated by Archimedes; as much of it, at least, as will suffice for the delight of a mind that loves geometry. For proofs complete and perfect in every respect must be sought from the little books of Archimedes themselves, if anyone does not shrink from their thorny reading.

Let me, however, dwell a little on certain places that Archimedes did not touch, so that the more learned too may find something to help and delight them.
