# Addition: On Newton's Attraction and the Celestial Matter

::: {.editorial-note}
English translation of the 1690 Leiden printing, made for Plain Old Text. Each paragraph translates the corresponding paragraph of the original. "It" in the first sentence is the excess of the Earth's equatorial over its polar radius, which Huygens has just found to be 1/578 of the radius EC.
:::

[]{.page n=159}Mr. Newton finds it 1/230 of EC, and so the shape of the Earth departs much further from the spherical, using for this a quite different computation, which I shall not examine here, since in any case I do not agree with a principle he assumes in this calculation and elsewhere: namely, that all the small parts that can be imagined in two or more different bodies attract one another, or tend to approach one another mutually. This I cannot admit, because I believe I see clearly that the cause of such an attraction cannot be explained by any principle of mechanics, nor by the rules of motion. Nor am I persuaded either of the necessity of the mutual attraction of whole bodies, having shown that, even if there were no Earth, bodies would still tend towards a centre by what we call their gravity.

[]{.page n=160}So I have nothing against the *vis centripeta*, as Mr. Newton calls it, by which he makes the planets gravitate towards the Sun and the Moon towards the Earth; on the contrary, I readily agree to it: because not only do we know from experience that there is such a kind of attraction or impulsion in nature, but it can also be explained by the laws of motion, as was seen in what I wrote above about gravity. For nothing prevents the cause of this *vis centripeta* towards the Sun from being like the one that pushes the bodies we call heavy to fall towards the Earth. I had long since imagined that the spherical shape of the Sun might be produced in the same way as the one which, in my view, produces the roundness of the Earth; but I had not extended the action of gravity to such great distances, as from the Sun to the planets, or from the Earth to the Moon, because Mr. Descartes' vortices, which had once seemed to me very likely and which were still in my mind, stood in the way. Nor had I thought of this regular decrease of gravity, namely that it is inversely as the squares of the distances from the centre: a new and very remarkable property of gravity, whose reason is well worth seeking. But seeing now from Mr. Newton's demonstrations that, by supposing such a gravity towards the Sun, decreasing in that ratio, it balances the centrifugal forces of the planets so well, and produces exactly the effect of the elliptical motion that Kepler had guessed and confirmed by observation, I can hardly doubt that these hypotheses about gravity are true, or that Mr. Newton's system, in so far as it rests on them, is true as well. Which should seem all the more probable because it resolves several difficulties that were troublesome in the vortices []{.page n=161}supposed by Descartes. We now see how the eccentricities of the planets can stay constantly the same; why the planes of their orbits do not merge, but keep their different inclinations to the plane of the ecliptic; and why the planes of all these orbits necessarily pass through the Sun. How the motions of the planets can speed up and slow down by the degrees observed in them, which could hardly be so if they swam in a vortex round the Sun. Finally we see how comets can cross our system. For since it has been known that they often enter the region of the planets, it was hard to conceive how they could sometimes move against the motion of the vortex, which was strong enough to carry the planets along. But by Mr. Newton's doctrine this doubt too is removed, since nothing prevents comets from travelling elliptical paths round the Sun like the planets, only longer ones and of a shape further from the circular; and so these bodies may have their periodic returns, as some philosophers and astronomers, ancient and modern, had imagined.

There is only this difficulty, that Mr. Newton, in rejecting Descartes' vortices, wants the celestial spaces to contain only a very rare matter, so that the planets and comets may meet the less hindrance in their course. With this rarity granted, it does not seem possible to explain either the action of gravity or that of light, at least in the ways I have used. To examine this point, then, I say that the ethereal matter can be taken to be rare in two ways: either its particles are far apart, with much void between them; or they touch, but the texture of each is rare and []{.page n=162}interspersed with many small empty spaces. As for the void, I admit it without difficulty, and I even believe it necessary for the motion of the small corpuscles among themselves, not being of the opinion of Mr. Descartes, who holds that extension alone makes the essence of body, but adding to it perfect hardness, which makes it impenetrable and incapable of being broken or chipped. Yet considering rarity of the first kind, I do not see how one could then account for gravity; and as for light, it seems to me wholly impossible, with such voids, to explain its prodigious speed, which must be six hundred thousand times greater than that of sound, by Mr. Rømer's demonstration, which I reported in the Treatise on Light. That is why I hold that such a rarity cannot suit the celestial spaces.

It is more plausible to conceive it the other way, because the particles can then touch, as I supposed them in that Treatise, and yet, because of the lightness of their texture, offer very little resistance to the motion of the planets. For who knows how far nature can go in making hard bodies with little matter, above all if very small and slender, or even hollow, particles can be infinitely strong. But I believe that, rarity aside, the great agitation of the ethereal matter may contribute much to its penetrability. For if the small motion of the particles of water makes it liquid, and of much less resistance to bodies swimming in it than sand or some very fine powder, must not a matter more subtle and infinitely more agitated be all the easier to penetrate?

However that may be, we see that nature does not lack the skill to make spaces in which bodies move with very little resistance; for this ap[]{.page n=163}pears from what our hands feel in the air, and still more from the experiments made in glass vessels from which all the air has been drawn, where the lightest feather falls with the same speed as a lead ball. And if anyone wished to maintain that this comes from the great rarity of the matter remaining in this space emptied of air, I would reply on the contrary that one sees there the effect of a matter that weighs very considerably, as was seen in the experiment reported above.

As for Mr. Newton's reasoning in Prop. 6 of Book 3, to prove the extreme rarity of the ether, namely that the weights of bodies are as the quantities of matter they contain, and that, this being so, if the spaces of the air or the ether were as full of matter as gold and silver, these metals would not fall in them, because a solid body that has no greater specific gravity than a fluid cannot sink in it: I say that I agree that the weights of bodies follow the quantities of their matter, and I have even proved it in this present Discourse. But I have also shown that the bodies we call heavy may well have their gravity impressed on them by the centrifugal force of a matter that does not itself gravitate towards the centre of the Earth, because of its circular and very rapid motion, but tends to move away from it. This matter, then, may very well fill all the space round the Earth that other corpuscles do not occupy, without hindering the descent of the bodies called heavy; being, on the contrary, the only cause that compels them to it. It would be another matter if one supposed that gravity were an inherent quality of corporeal matter. But I do not believe Mr. Newton would agree to that, because such a hypothesis would take us far from mathematical or mechanical principles.
