*Draft. Written as a starting point; every claim not taken from the letter itself still needs checking against the literature.*

Kepler wrote this letter at Sagan, in Silesia, where he had moved in 1628 to work for Albrecht von Wallenstein, Duke of Friedland and, since 1628, of Sagan. The letter is a purchase order wrapped in a confession of nerves. Kepler is sending two young men, a compositor and a pressman, to Leipzig to buy a printing press, to have type cast and to order paper, and he asks Philipp Müller, professor of mathematics at Leipzig, to advance the money and to keep an eye on the bargaining.

## A press for Tycho's observations

The stated purpose is "the edition of Brahe's observations". Kepler had held Tycho Brahe's observation records since Tycho's death in 1601, and the *Rudolphine Tables* (Ulm, 1627), which were built on them, were finally out. Printing the observations themselves was the obvious next step, and owning a press would free Kepler from the printers of Linz and Ulm, with whom he had struggled.[^1] He hedges even here: he may only "pretend to begin" the edition, or actually begin it.

The observations did not appear in Kepler's lifetime. He died at Regensburg in November 1630, a year after this letter.[^2]

## Money held in trust

The middle of the letter is about cash. Jacob Bartsch, Kepler's assistant, who was to marry his daughter Susanna in 1630, had deposited a hundred joachimstalers with Müller, and Müller already held thirty-six of Kepler's. Kepler quotes Bartsch's own words to show that the money is available, admits that Bartsch wanted to be consulted first, and asks Müller to spend it anyway, since Bartsch is about to leave for the Frankfurt fair. It is a small window onto how scholars of the period moved money: deposits with a trusted colleague, receipts, letters of exchange between merchants of different towns.

## "A nursling of Mars"

The opening is the most quoted part of the letter, and the most characteristic. Kepler calls himself *Martis alumnus et tantum non pullus*, "a nursling of Mars, indeed all but his chick". The joke works on two levels. Mars is the god of war, and Kepler is writing in the middle of the Thirty Years' War, in the service of its most famous general. But Mars was also the planet that made Kepler's name: the *Astronomia nova* (1609) was built on Tycho's observations of Mars.

He ends on the same note. If Wallenstein's fortunes collapse, Müller is to stop the purchase; the press is only safe as long as its patron is. Wallenstein was dismissed by the emperor in August 1630, and Kepler left Sagan that autumn on the journey that ended with his death at Regensburg.
